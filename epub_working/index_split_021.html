◇ Note. The four chambers of Sheol. 1 Enoch 22 is the earliest text to teach a divided afterlife, with separate compartments for the righteous, the suffering wicked, the un-judged sinners, and the murdered seeking justice. This is the structural ancestor of Greek Hades-with-divisions (compare Plutarch, De facie) and Christian Purgatory. In the New Testament, Luke 16:19–31 (the rich man and Lazarus) and 1 Peter 3:19–20 (the spirits in prison) presuppose something close to this geography. The Aramaic of 4QEnᵉ ii corresponds closely to the Greek of Codex Panopolitanus.

The tree of wisdom — a parallel Eden. 1 Enoch 32:3–6 transposes the Eden tradition: there is a fragrant tree on a mountain at the eastern ends of the earth, identified by the angel Raphael as the tree of wisdom from which Adam and Eve ate. This is independent of — not a retelling of — the Genesis 3 narrative; it shows that multiple interpretive traditions about Eden and the trees of paradise circulated in Second Temple Judaism. Jubilees 8 and the Qumran Genesis Apocryphon know related geographies. The mountain-tree complex influenced Christian apocalyptic geography (cf. Revelation 22:2).

◇ Note. Apocalyptic ascent vocabulary. Whirlwind-ascent is the technical machinery of Jewish apocalyptic visions: Elijah (2 Kings 2:11), Ezekiel (Ezek 3:12, 8:3), Paul (2 Cor 12:2-4), and the seer of 4 Ezra 14 all rise in or with whirlwinds. In Greek the term is lailaŬs (λαῖλαψ) or pneuma; here in 1 Enoch the chosen verb across the Greek and Aramaic witnesses preserves the active-passive ambiguity — the seer is taken up rather than ascending by his own power. This grammatical detail is what distinguishes apocalyptic ascent from later Hekhalot self-induced ascent practice.

◇ Note. The chaos beyond. Enoch’s tour reaches the place "beyond cosmic order" — a chaotic region where the disobedient stars are imprisoned (21:3–6) and the rebel Watchers held in their place of judgment. The geography is theologically significant: 1 Enoch envisions creation as having boundaries beyond which structured cosmos gives way to chaos, and within those chaotic regions divine judgment incarcerates the cosmic rebels. The principle that judgment-spaces exist within (or beyond) creation rather than constituting a separate metaphysical realm is characteristic of Second Temple cosmology and informs the NT’s eschatological geography directly: 2 Pet 2:4 ("God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them down to hell [tartaroō]") and Jude 6 ("kept in everlasting chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day") draw on this 1 Enoch geographical framework. The verse opens one of apocalyptic literature’s most influential cosmic-prison images.

'And from thence I went to another place, which was still more horrible than the former, and I saw a horrible thing: a great fire there which burnt and blazed, and the place was cleft as far as the abyss, being full of great descending columns of fire' — the Tartaros-Gehenna vision. Enoch sees the place of final punishment for the wicked, distinguished from the prison of the rebel angels in 18:14 by its more-horrible character. The fire descending in columns Tewahedo reads as the inverse of the Pentecostal tongues of fire descending in blessing (Acts 2:3): the same fire-imagery serves either as gift (when received by the righteous Spirit) or as judgment (when meeting the unrighteous).

◇ Note. The angelic prison. The verse names the location: "the prison of the angels, and here they will be imprisoned for ever." The doctrine of an eternal angelic prison — that fallen angels are not annihilated but bound permanently — has had immense theological afterlife. NT writings cite it explicitly (Jude 6, "angels… kept in everlasting chains under darkness"; 2 Pet 2:4, the same imagery; Rev 20:1–3, the binding of Satan in the abyss for a thousand years). Christian theological tradition develops the doctrine into the medieval picture of permanent angelic damnation (cf. Aquinas, Summa 1.64) — drawing both on these NT texts and on this 1 Enoch foundation. The verse is one of the few specific cosmic-judicial verses in 1 Enoch with so direct an inheritance into Christian dogma; the eternal-binding-of-rebel-angels theology that runs through almost every form of Christian belief originates structurally from this image.

◇ Note. The geography of judgment. Enoch’s tour-route is geographically schematic: he moves between named cosmic locations at the "ends of the earth," each associated with a particular cosmic function (winds, fire, judgment, paradise). The "ends of the earth" geography assumes a flat-disc cosmology with sharply bounded peripheral regions where ordinary terrestrial geography gives way to cosmic-cosmological structures. The pattern derives from broader ANE world-pictures (the Babylonian world-map, the Egyptian dwat) but receives specifically Israelite-apocalyptic theological elaboration here. The "western fire" Enoch is about to see (23:4) connects to broader Hellenistic-Jewish traditions of the western horizon as the entry to the underworld (cf. Greek Hades, the Egyptian western funerary tradition). Enoch’s tour is therefore not purely fantastic geography but draws on widely shared ancient cosmological imagination.

◇ Note. The seven mountains and the throne. Enoch sees seven mountains, with the seventh as central, throne-shaped, surrounded by fragrant trees. The image draws on multiple traditions: the cosmic mountain at the center of the world (a widely shared ANE myth-pattern, with parallels in Mesopotamian, Canaanite, and biblical traditions — Mount Zion, Mount Sinai, Mount Olympus, Mount Hermon), the divine throne-mountain (cf. Ezek 28:14, "the holy mountain of God"), and the seven-mountain geographical schema specific to apocalyptic cosmology. Rev 17:9 ("the seven mountains on which the woman sits") draws on related but distinct seven-mountain traditions. The seventh-as-throne pattern picks up the broader theological premium on the seventh in seven-fold structures (seven days, seven branches, seven seals) and identifies the climactic, central position with divine occupancy. The chapter establishes a cosmic geography that is also liturgical-theological geography.

◇ Note. The fragrant tree. The unique tree at the central mountain — fragrant beyond all other fragrances, with leaves and blossoms eternally lasting — is identified in the next chapter (25:4–6) as the eschatological Tree of Life, which the righteous will be permitted to taste at the final judgment. The image draws directly on Gen 2–3 (the Tree of Life in Eden, lost at the fall), reframed eschatologically: what was lost will be restored at the consummation. Rev 2:7, 22:2, and 22:14 ("the tree of life") explicitly cite this 1 Enoch tradition through its NT mediation. The chapter is one of the foundational texts of Christian eschatological imagery — the recovery of paradisal access, the eternal life of the resurrected righteous, and the central place of the divine presence at the eschaton. The tree’s fragrance — not merely visual or alimentary but olfactory — is one of 1 Enoch’s most distinctive sensory-theological images.

◇ Note. Raguel’s explanation. Raguel — one of the seven archangels named in ch 20 — explains the western fire: it is "the fire of all the luminaries of heaven" pursuing them on their cosmic tracks. The image works at multiple levels: physically (the heavenly bodies move through what looks like fire), cosmologically (the cosmic-fire substrate of the heavens, an idea also present in Stoic philosophy and Ptolemaic astronomy), and theologically (fire as the visible substance of divine presence and judgment, recurring throughout OT theophany and later apocalyptic). The chapter is one of 1 Enoch’s clearer examples of the genre’s integration of cosmological speculation with mythological figure: the cosmic geography Enoch reports is partly observable astronomical phenomenon (luminaries moving through bright sky), partly mythologized in narrative form (named angels guiding seers through named regions). The genre allows both registers simultaneously without seeming to feel the tension we might.

◇ Note. The throne-mountain. Michael’s explanation that the central mountain is "His throne, where the Holy Great One, the Lord of Glory, the Eternal King, will sit when He shall come down to visit the earth with goodness" is one of 1 Enoch’s most influential Christological-eschatological images. The verse merges two distinct OT traditions: the divine-throne theology of Isa 6 and Ezek 1 (the seer beholding Yahweh enthroned on high) and the divine-visitation theology (cf. Mic 1:3, "Yahweh comes forth out of his place, and will come down"). The eschatological synthesis — divine throne at the end of days, when God descends visibly to judge the earth — becomes structural in apocalyptic literature and in NT eschatology (cf. Rev 20:11–15, "I saw a great white throne, and him who sat on it"). The 1 Enoch image of the throne-mountain at the cosmic center as eschatological judgment-seat is one of the most direct intertestamental contributions to NT apocalyptic imagery.

◇ 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) (-200). 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1-36) (c. 200 BC)

The angel Michael explains the mountain on which the tree of life grows: 'This high mountain which thou hast seen, whose summit is like the throne of God, is His throne, where the Holy Great One, the Lord of Glory, the Eternal King, will sit, when He shall come down to visit the earth with goodness' — the throne-mountain of the eschatological visitation. The mountain serves simultaneously as God's throne AND as the place of His descent to creation: in the eschaton the transcendent and immanent are reunited geographically. Tewahedo theological geography preserves this throne-mountain imagery; Mount Zion (and by extension every consecrated church building) participates in this eschatological throne-mountain typology.

◇ Note. The Tree of Life eaten. The chapter promises the righteous access to the eschatological Tree of Life — its fruit "for food to the elect," transplanted to the holy place at the eschaton. The image fulfils the loss of Eden (Gen 3:22–24, where Adam was barred from the Tree of Life) by promising eschatological restoration. Rev 2:7 ("to him who overcomes I will grant to eat from the tree of life, which is in the paradise of God") and Rev 22:14 ("blessed are those who wash their robes, that they may have the right to the tree of life") draw directly on this 1 Enoch tradition. The transplantation motif — the Tree relocated from its original cosmic-mountain setting to the eschatological temple — adds a typological layer: the holy place that will receive the Tree is the new-Zion temple of the eschaton. The verse is one of the foundational texts of Christian "paradise restored" eschatology and shapes the imagery of the heavenly city in Revelation directly.

◇ Note. The earth’s navel. Enoch’s arrival at the cosmic center is geographically suggestive: 1 Enoch identifies the location with what later Jewish tradition will call the tabbur ha-aretz — the "navel of the earth," the cosmic center-point. Rabbinic tradition (Tanḥuma Qedoshim 10) identifies the navel-of-the-earth with Mount Zion specifically, and the chapter’s subsequent identification of the central mountain (probably Zion) with the temple-mount aligns with this identification. The "navel" terminology has Hellenistic parallels (the Greek omphalos at Delphi, the famous oracle-stone marking Apollo’s temple as the world’s center) — the cosmic-center concept is widely shared across ancient cultures. 1 Enoch’s specifically Jewish version locates the navel-of-the-earth at Jerusalem and links it to the divine throne, the Tree of Life, and eschatological judgment. The verse is one of the foundational texts of the entire later Jewish-Christian theology of Jerusalem-as-cosmic-center.

◇ Note. The cursed valley. The valley between the holy mountain and the cursed land is identified in the chapter (27:2–3) as the place of judgment for the wicked at the end of days. As noted, modern scholarship has read this as 1 Enoch’s eschatological reframing of the Valley of Hinnom (Gei Hinnom) — the actual ravine south of Jerusalem where child-sacrifice occurred in the monarchic period (cf. 2 Kgs 23:10, Jer 7:31, 19:5–6, 32:35). Jeremiah explicitly transmutes the historical-geographical valley into eschatological judgment-image (Jer 7:32, 19:6, "the Valley of Slaughter"); 1 Enoch develops this transmutation further; the NT inherits the developed concept as Gehenna (Matt 5:22, 5:29–30, 10:28, 18:9, 23:15, 23:33; Mark 9:43, 45, 47; Luke 12:5; James 3:6). The verse is therefore one of the linking texts in the development from real Hinnom-valley to eschatological Gehenna — a transmutation that runs from OT historical geography through Second Temple apocalyptic to NT theology.

◇ 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) (-200). 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1-36) (c. 200 BC)

The angel explains the cursed Valley of Hinnom: 'This accursed valley is for those who are accursed for ever: here shall all the accursed be gathered together who utter with their lips against the Lord unseemly words and of His glory speak hard things' — the Gehenna eschatology grounded in geographical Hinnom Valley outside Jerusalem. The valley where idolatrous Israel sacrificed children to Molech becomes the Mäṣḥafä Hēnok's typological location of the eschatological gathering of the condemned. New Testament 'Gehenna' (12 occurrences) presupposes this Enochic geographical-eschatological identification; Tewahedo eschatology preserves the Hinnom-Gehenna typological continuity.

◇ Note. The judgment spectacle. The chapter’s vision is theologically stark: in the last days the righteous will see the wicked’s punishment as spectacle, witnessing divine justice fulfilled in their sight. The motif — eschatological judgment witnessed by the righteous — runs through Second Temple literature and reappears in NT eschatology (cf. Luke 13:28, "you shall see Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets, in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves cast out"; Rev 14:10, "tormented in the presence of the holy angels and in the presence of the Lamb"). The principle the chapter encodes — that final justice is not merely accomplished but visibly demonstrated to the righteous — has been theologically influential and theologically troubling in equal measure. The 1 Enoch tradition encodes both elements clearly: the satisfying-justice register (the wicked finally accountable) and the difficult-spectacle register (suffering witnessed as vindication).

◇ Note. The aromatic east. Enoch’s tour now turns east of the cosmic center, into a region of fragrant trees and plants — beginning what becomes a sustained catalog of aromatic specimens (chs 28–32). The geographical orientation reflects ancient Mediterranean-Levantine assumptions about the eastern provenance of incense and exotic spices (cf. the wise men "from the east" bringing frankincense and myrrh in Matt 2:11, drawing on the same eastern-aromatic geographical-mythological tradition). The chapters’ aromatic geography is partly real-economic (Arabian frankincense, Indian cinnamon, etc., did reach the Mediterranean from the east) and partly mythologically idealized (paradisal eastern regions in the imaginative geography of the period). 1 Enoch’s catalog draws on this shared imaginative space and converts it into theological geography.

◇ Note. The water-cycle in apocalyptic vision. The chapter’s observation that the rushing watercourse causes clouds and dew to rise is one of 1 Enoch’s small naturalistic-cosmological moments. The water-cycle — water rising as vapor, returning as rain — was understood empirically in the ANE and is reflected in OT wisdom literature (cf. Job 36:27–28, Eccl 1:7, Sirach 39:22). The chapter integrates the natural observation into theological geography: the cosmic structure includes not only static features (mountains, rivers) but operative cycles that perpetually maintain the cosmos. The integration of natural-cyclic phenomena with theological geography becomes one of the structural features of 1 Enoch’s cosmology, especially in the Astronomical Book (chs 72–82) where solar, lunar, and stellar cycles are catalogued in detail. The chapter is one of 1 Enoch’s clearest moments of natural observation interpreted within apocalyptic-theological frame.

◇ Note. The eastern aromatic continuation. The chapter continues the eastward tour through aromatic regions, with each successive location adding to a catalog of fragrances. The literary effect is partly cumulative-rhetorical: the reader is overwhelmed by the abundance and variety of paradisal aromatic resources, registering the cosmic richness of the divine creation. The catalogs also have a documentary function — preserving information about the actual aromatics traded across the ancient Mediterranean: frankincense (Boswellia spp., from southern Arabia), myrrh (Commiphora myrrha, Yemen-Somalia), cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum, originally Indian), nard (Nardostachys, Himalayan), and several others 1 Enoch will name in subsequent chapters. The chapter is one of the small but valuable Second Temple windows on the actual Levantine-Indian Ocean spice trade of the period, framed within apocalyptic theological geography.

◇ Note. Frankincense and myrrh. The two named aromatics — frankincense (Hebrew levonah) and myrrh (mor) — are the most theologically loaded specimens in the OT incense-economy. Frankincense was a required ingredient in the sacred incense (Exod 30:34); myrrh was an ingredient in the holy anointing oil (Exod 30:23). Both were among the most expensive trade goods of the ANE. The NT presents them at Christ’s nativity (Matt 2:11) and at his burial (John 19:39, Nicodemus’ myrrh-and-aloes mixture for embalming) as the framing aromatics of his earthly mission. 1 Enoch’s placement of these specifically named aromatics in the eschatological-paradisal landscape registers their theological weight: they are not just expensive luxuries but the substance-stuff of holy worship. The verse encodes one of the small but significant continuities between Second Temple cosmological imagination and NT Christological symbolism.

◇ Note. The watered valley. The chapter introduces a valley filled with water amid the fragrant trees — a composition of rich vegetation, abundant water, and aromatic plants that completes the paradisal landscape. The detail is theologically suggestive: the four elements that constitute the eschatological paradise (mountains, water, fragrance, abundant vegetation) approximate Eden of Gen 2 (rivers, trees, fragrant plants in 2:9, 10–14). 1 Enoch’s apocalyptic geography is consistently restorative — the eastern paradise the seer encounters is the lost Eden recovered in the apocalyptic-cosmic register. The same restorative logic underwrites Rev 22:1–2 ("a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne… in the midst of its street and on either side of the river was the tree of life"), where eschatological Jerusalem is depicted with the same Eden-recovery motif. 1 Enoch is one of the structural sources for Revelation’s paradisal closing image.

◇ Note. Cinnamon in apocalyptic geography. Cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum) is one of the longest-traded ANE luxury aromatics, sourced originally from Sri Lanka and southern India and reaching the Mediterranean via Arabian intermediaries from at least the second millennium BCE. Its cost made it a marker of luxury (cf. Prov 7:17, "I have perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon"; Song 4:14, the lover’s garden including cinnamon; Rev 18:13, the trade-cargo of fallen Babylon including "cinnamon, and odors, and ointments"). The chapter’s mention is one of 1 Enoch’s moments where actual spice-trade geography enters apocalyptic vision: the cinnamon Enoch sees growing in eastern paradisal regions corresponds, mythologically transformed, to the real Indian-Sri Lankan cinnamon reaching Levantine markets. The chapter participates in a wider cultural imagination linking the eastern paradise with the eastern source of expensive aromatics, an imaginative geography that informs Magi traditions and NT iconography directly.

◇ Note. The named gum-resins. "Sarara" and "galbanum" are gum-resins — galbanum (ferula gummosa) was specifically an ingredient in the sacred incense (Exod 30:34, "stacte, and onycha, and galbanum"). The mention of galbanum in 1 Enoch’s eschatological-aromatic catalog is theologically calibrated: this is not just any pleasant scent but specifically a sacred-incense ingredient, registering that the eschatological paradise contains the very substances out of which Israelite worship is made. The pattern is consistent through the chapters’ aromatic catalog: the spices Enoch sees in paradise are precisely the spices Israel uses in temple worship. The implication is theologically rich — temple worship traffics in eschatological substance, and the worshipper using temple-incense is, in some symbolic register, already drawing on paradisal supply. The 1 Enoch tradition encodes a doctrine of cult as anticipatory participation in the eschaton.

◇ Note. The supreme fragrance. The chapter ends with a small superlative — the burnt resin smelled "sweeter than any fragrant odour." The construction is one of 1 Enoch’s recurrent rhetorical devices: cumulatively superlative descriptions register cosmic-eschatological excess, where each item is more than the previous. The same rhetorical pattern recurs at 14:18 (Yahweh’s throne "more brilliant than the sun"), 24:4 (the fragrant tree with "fragrance beyond all fragrance"), and elsewhere. The cumulative effect is theologically purposeful: the apocalyptic landscape exceeds anything human experience contains, and ordinary descriptive language strains under the weight. The verse is one of many small markers of the genre’s self-understanding — apocalyptic vision is not merely informative but performatively stretches the categories the seer has available, communicating the otherness of the cosmic-eschatological order through linguistic excess.

◇ Note. The Garden of Righteousness. Enoch’s arrival at "the Garden of Righteousness" — explicitly identified with Eden — is one of 1 Enoch’s most direct re-engagements with Genesis. The garden contains the Tree of Wisdom (32:3, distinct from but parallel to the Tree of Life of ch 24–25): "from this tree the man and woman of old ate and acquired knowledge, and were driven out of the garden." The interpretive move is theologically dense: 1 Enoch identifies the Genesis "tree of the knowledge of good and evil" specifically with wisdom-acquisition and treats the ancestral expulsion as the cost of premature wisdom-grasping. The Wisdom of Solomon (10:1–2) and Sirach (24:23–29) develop similar Wisdom-tree theology in different directions. The chapter is one of 1 Enoch’s most consequential single re-readings of Genesis: the fall is reframed as wisdom-violation rather than as obedience-failure simpliciter. The verse is one of Second Temple Judaism’s most direct windows on its own evolving interpretation of Eden.

◇ Note. The eastern fauna. Enoch’s eastern tour now turns to the cosmic ends of the earth, where remarkable animal and bird life is found. The image draws on a wider ANE imagination of marvelous fauna at the world’s edges (cf. Strabo, Pliny on the legendary creatures of India and Africa; the Behemoth and Leviathan of Job 40–41 and 1 Enoch 60). The detail is theologically more than zoological: the diversity and strangeness of cosmic-edge creatures register the abundance and creativity of divine craftsmanship beyond the ordinary terrestrial range. The chapter participates in a theological aesthetics of cosmic surplus — God’s creation is not exhausted by what the human inhabitant of Palestine sees daily, but extends in unexpected forms toward the cosmic peripheries.

◇ Note. The stellar portals. Enoch’s reported counting of the heavenly portals through which the stars proceed introduces the technical-astronomical theology that the Astronomical Book (chs 72–82) will systematize. The portal-theory of stellar appearance — that stars enter and exit visible heaven through fixed gates at the eastern and western horizon — is consistent with Babylonian astronomical theory of the period (cf. the MUL.APIN tablets, mid-second millennium BCE) and represents 1 Enoch’s integration of ANE technical astronomy with apocalyptic theology. The verse functions as bridge to the Astronomical Book’s detailed material; the seer’s function as cosmic surveyor includes recording technical-astronomical detail, treating the mathematical-observational data of star-positions as theologically significant. The chapter encodes one of 1 Enoch’s most distinctive theological-cosmological commitments: the rationality of cosmic order is itself revelation.

◇ Note. The northern mechanism. Enoch’s northward tour reveals "a great and glorious device" — the Aramaic likely ’ata ("sign, marvel"), the Greek theama ("spectacle, sight"). The vocabulary suggests a technical-mechanical structure rather than mere landscape feature: the cosmic device by which winds are released. The image of cosmic machinery — gates, portals, mechanisms regulated by angelic supervisors — is one of 1 Enoch’s recurring images. The Astronomical Book elaborates this thoroughly with calendar-mechanism details. The image registers a theologically distinctive view: the cosmos is engineered with precision, and the angelic order maintains the engineering. The pattern departs from purely organicist or animistic cosmologies and inserts a strong techno-cosmological register that influences subsequent apocalyptic-mystical traditions (the Hekhalot literature’s detailed cosmic-architectural descriptions descend partially from this Enochic tradition).

◇ Note. The two-thirds violent winds. The chapter notes that of the three northern wind-portals, only one releases beneficent wind; the other two release "violence and affliction on the earth, and they blow with violence." The detail registers ancient observation of wind-character: northern winds were associated with cold, storm, and harsh weather, while specific wind-quarters could bring either benefit or destruction. The 1 Enoch theological framework treats these patterns as cosmically calibrated — not random meteorological variation but functional differentiation of cosmic resources for distinct purposes. The same theological framework reappears in Job 38–41 (where Yahweh’s questions to Job assume divinely-ordered weather phenomena) and in OT psalmody (Pss 104, 147 on weather as divine work). The chapter is one of 1 Enoch’s clearest cosmological moments combining empirical weather-observation with theological-functional explanation.

◇ Note. The west portals. The chapter records three western portals corresponding to the eastern three (described earlier in the cosmic tour). The symmetry — three portals at each cardinal direction, twelve total — encodes a cosmic-mathematical structure that the Astronomical Book of 1 Enoch will systematize. The pattern of twelve sectors of heaven (three per cardinal direction) becomes one of the recurring cosmological-mathematical schemas of Second Temple Judaism, with parallels in Babylonian astronomy (the twelve-monthly division of the zodiacal year) and surviving in later Jewish liturgical and calendrical traditions. The verse is one of 1 Enoch’s clearest portrayals of cosmic ordering: even the brief one-verse chapter encapsulates the whole cosmographic principle of symmetric four-fold structure with internal three-fold subdivision. The mathematical framework underwrites both the natural-phenomena explanations (winds, rain) and the calendrical-festival systems that depend on them.

◇ Note. The southern portals. The chapter completes the four-cardinal cosmic tour with the southern portals, source of dew, rain, and wind. The chapter’s placement at the end of the Book of Watchers section is structurally significant: Enoch has now toured all four cardinal directions, the cosmic center, and the boundaries between order and chaos. The book-section closes with this comprehensive cosmic-geographical mapping, having moved from Watchers’ rebellion (chs 6–11) through cosmic prison (ch 21) to cosmic structure (chs 17–36). The literary architecture is sophisticated: a moral-historical crisis frames the cosmic tour, with the tour functioning as theological response to the crisis (the cosmos remains structured even when the watchers rebelled; God remains in control even when angels fall). The chapter is the final geographical waypoint of the tour-section.

◇ Note. The stellar courses. The chapter ends the Watchers section with a final astronomical observation: the stars passing through the southern portals run their courses to the west on prescribed paths. The verse registers what the entire Watchers section has been arguing: cosmic phenomena follow appointed schedules under divine ordering, and the seer’s function is to document this ordering as theological evidence. The image of stars on prescribed paths recurs throughout Enochic literature (cf. 2:1, the chapter’s parallel; 41:5, "I saw the chambers of the sun and moon… how their work and rotation are appointed"; 43:1–2, Enoch counting all the stars by name). The chapter concludes the Book of Watchers (chs 1–36) on a note of cosmic order achieved and witnessed; the next major section (chs 37–71, the Book of Parables) will turn from cosmic observation to eschatological proclamation.

◇ Note. The Zion identification. The chapter’s "holy mountain" — central to the cosmos, with surrounding valleys, streams flowing east, and a deeply cursed valley to the southeast — has been read by most modern scholarship as 1 Enoch’s mythological-geographic representation of Jerusalem and its surroundings. The "accursed valley" of 27:1–3 is identified with the Valley of Hinnom (Hebrew Gei Hinnom, hence Greek Gehenna) — the actual ravine south of Jerusalem associated with child sacrifice in the OT period and used as an image of eschatological punishment in Second Temple and NT literature. The chapter therefore performs a remarkable interpretive move: it transmutes the actual geography of Jerusalem and its surroundings into apocalyptic-cosmic terms, with each visible feature now bearing eschatological significance. The verse is the structural-geographic anchor of one of 1 Enoch’s most theologically influential image-clusters.

◇ Note. The east-west symmetry. The chapter explicitly notes the symmetry: the west portals correspond exactly in number to those Enoch had seen in the east (cf. ch 33). The east-west symmetry establishes that the cosmic structure is bilateral: stars and luminaries that rise in the east set in the west, with portals at each end through which they enter and exit visible heaven. The principle is consistent with observable astronomy and reflects basic ANE celestial-mechanics theory. The "†east†" cruxes (the Ge‘ez text marks textual uncertainty) registers a transmission problem: the Aramaic original, the Greek translation, and the Ge‘ez Ethiopic text all preserve slightly different readings here, with modern editors disagreeing about the original. The chapter is one of many places where the manuscript transmission of 1 Enoch shows visible textual instability — a reminder that we are reading a complex multi-stage text whose textual history is itself part of the scholarly study of the book.

◇ Note. Apocalyptic prologue. The Book of Parables (chs 37–71) opens as a “second vision,” distinct in genre from the Watcher narrative of the first 36 chapters. The wisdom-discourse framing aligns this material with the Wisdom corpus (Sirach, Wisdom of Solomon) rather than visionary apocalyptic narrative. The composition is widely dated to the late first century BCE or early first century CE — too late to appear in the Qumran library, which is why the Book of Parables alone among the five Enochic sections is absent from the Aramaic Dead Sea Scrolls of 1 Enoch. Its Christological vocabulary (Son of Man, Elect One, Righteous One) makes it pivotal for understanding second-temple Jewish messianism.

◇ Note. Structure of the Parables. The three parables are chs 38–44, 45–57, and 58–69 — sometimes called the “Similitudes” in older scholarship. Chs 70–71 form an appendix recounting Enoch’s ascension and his identification with the Son of Man, a pivotal text for understanding how the figure’s referent was understood in the tradition. The three-parable structure parallels other apocalyptic compositions that use triadic vision-cycles (4 Ezra, 2 Baruch). Nickelsburg’s 1 Enoch 2 (Hermeneia, 2012) gives the most extensive recent treatment of the editorial seams.

◇ Note. The Righteous One. “The Righteous One” is one of three titles applied to a heavenly figure across the Parables, alongside “the Elect One” and “the Son of Man.” Acts 3:14 applies the title to Jesus in Peter’s sermon, and James 5:6 echoes the language of the suffering Righteous One being condemned. Whether Acts is consciously drawing on the Enochic vocabulary or using a shared messianic register is debated; the Parables were certainly known in early Christian circles, since Jude 14–15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 directly.

◇ Note. Better not to have been born. The phrase has an exact verbal echo in Mark 14:21 / Matthew 26:24 (concerning Judas): “It would have been better for that man if he had not been born.” The Parables likely supply the immediate idiomatic background. The motif itself is older — Jeremiah 20:14–18 and Job 3 both wish for non-birth — but the precise formulation is a marker that the Synoptic apocalyptic vocabulary draws specifically on the Enochic register rather than the older Hebrew tradition.

◇ Note. An interpolated Noachic fragment. 39:1–2 is widely identified as a fragment from a hypothetical “Book of Noah” incorporated into 1 Enoch by an editor; Charles’ edition marks it with textual brackets. The reference to descending angelic children is a brief inversion of the Watchers narrative (chs 6–11) that does not fit the surrounding First Parable. Several other passages in 1 Enoch (54:7–55:2; 65–69:25; 106–107) are similarly identified as Noachic interpolations. Milik (1976) and VanderKam (1984) both reconstructed a hypothetical lost Book of Noah from these fragments.

◇ Note. Angelic intercession. The motif of righteous angels interceding for humans before the divine throne is a Second Temple development of the older tradition of prophetic intercession (Abraham at Sodom, Moses at Sinai, Samuel for Israel). It is paralleled in Tobit 12:12–15 (Raphael presenting prayers to God) and in the Qumran liturgical fragments. In the New Testament, Hebrews 7:25 transposes the role onto the exalted Christ; 1 Timothy 2:5 narrows mediation to a single human-divine figure. The Parables preserve the intermediate stage where multiple angelic intercessors are still active.

◇ Note. The four archangels. The four-archangel scheme (Michael, Raphael, Gabriel, Phanuel — named in 40:9) is one of the earliest extant articulations of this canonical group, narrowed from the seven of 1 Enoch 20. The number four reflects the cardinal-direction symbolism of Ezekiel’s four living creatures and the four-camp encampment of Numbers 2. Phanuel (sometimes Sariel or Uriel in parallel lists) is replaced by Uriel in later Christian and rabbinic traditions; the variation reflects the fluidity of the angelological tradition before its medieval codification.

'And on the four sides of the Lord of Spirits I saw four presences, different from those that sleep not, and I learnt their names: for the angel who went with me made known to me their names' — four-archangel theology of presence (parallel to but distinct from the seven-archangel list at 20:1). Tewahedo iconography of Christ surrounded by the four presences (often depicted on the tabot — the ark's tablets in qǝddǝst, the holy of holies) draws on this verse as canonical authority.

◇ Note. The Satans, plural. 1 Enoch is one of the earliest texts to use Satans in the plural — a class of accusers rather than a single figure. The Hebrew ha-satan of Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3 is a functional title (“the accuser”), not yet a proper name. The Parables’ plural “Satans” preserves the older sense of a class of forensic adversaries. The shift to a single proper-named Satan — Beliar, Mastema, the devil — emerges across Second Temple literature (Jubilees, Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs) and reaches its New Testament formulation as the singular cosmic adversary. The Parables sit at a hinge point in this development.

◇ 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) (-100). 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) Book of Parables (1 Enoch 37-71) (c. 100 BC)

'And after that I saw all the secrets of the heavens, and how the kingdom is divided, and how the actions of men are weighed in the balance' — the cosmic-balance image. Tewahedo eschatology of judgment as actual weighing (with Michael often depicted as the angel of the scales in church iconography) draws on this verse as scriptural anchor, paralleling Dan 5:27 and standing in lineage with the Egyptian Maat-tradition that Ethiopian iconography preserves through a Christianised filter.

◇ Note. Heavenly mansions. The “many dwelling places” trope of John 14:2 — long debated as a possible Enochic allusion — has its closest verbal parallel in this passage. Both depict a heavenly habitation reserved for the righteous, accessed in vision or after death. Whether the Johannine evangelist drew directly on the Parables or on a shared apocalyptic vocabulary is unresolvable, but the Parables provide the most plausible Jewish background for the image. The mansions trope is also picked up in 2 Enoch and 4 Ezra, suggesting it was well-circulated in the Hellenistic Jewish milieu.

◇ Note. Cosmological storehouses. The “chambers” or “storehouses” tradition draws on Job 38:22 (“the storehouses of the snow”) and Psalm 33:7, supplying an architectural metaphor for divine cosmic administration. The same vocabulary becomes elaborated in 2 Enoch 5–6 and in rabbinic Hekhalot literature, where each natural phenomenon is housed in its own chamber. The metaphor presupposes a participatory cosmos — winds, hail, dew — each element under angelic stewardship rather than abstract natural law. The architectural rendering of cosmology shapes 1 Enoch’s broader astronomical material in chs 72–82.

◇ Note. Wisdom rejected. One of the most theologically loaded short passages in Second Temple literature. Wisdom is personified as a feminine figure who descends to find a home among humans, fails, and returns to heaven — the inverse of the descent-and-indwelling pattern in Sirach 24, where Wisdom finds her dwelling in Israel. Most scholars read the Enochic version as a critical-prophetic counter-statement to the Sirach tradition: not “Wisdom found her home in Zion” but “Wisdom returned, having found no place.” The passage shaped the Johannine prologue’s “He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him” (John 1:11), which transposes Wisdom’s failed mission onto the Logos.

◇ Note. Unrighteousness succeeds. The mirror to verse 2: where Wisdom failed to find a dwelling among humans, Unrighteousness “found whom she sought not” — succeeded effortlessly. The pairing turns the chapter into a brief theodicy: human history is the inversion of cosmic order, and the disjuncture between heaven (where wisdom is housed) and earth (where unrighteousness flourishes) generates the apocalyptic crisis the rest of the Parables resolves. The image of unrighteousness as “rain in a desert / dew on a thirsty land” is bitterly ironic — the metaphors of nourishing wisdom in Deut 32:2 are appropriated for its opposite.

◇ Note. Uriel as guide of the luminaries. Uriel ("the light of God"/"fire of God") is one of the four archangels named in 1 Enoch 9:1 and 40:9, alongside Michael, Raphael, and Gabriel. His specialized portfolio is the cosmos’s celestial bodies — the orderly motions of sun, moon, and stars. The convention that each archangel has a domain (Michael over Israel, Raphael over healing, Gabriel over revelation, Uriel over luminaries) is firmly established in 1 Enoch and propagates into 2 Esdras (4 Ezra), the Greek Sirach (49:8), and rabbinic angelology. The Astronomical Book’s authority rests on Uriel’s direct dictation to Enoch.

◇ Note. The twelve solar portals. The cosmology imagines the dome of heaven pierced by twelve gates — six in the east where the sun rises and six in the west where it sets — through which the sun and moon pass each month. The mechanism is a literalization of seasonal change: as the sun shifts portals, day-length and weather change. Compare the Babylonian compendium MUL.APIN (early first millennium BCE), which uses a similar scheme of solar paths through "ways of Anu, Enlil, and Ea." VanderKam argues the Astronomical Book draws on Babylonian astronomy mediated through Aramaic-speaking scribes; the Aramaic Enoch fragments from Qumran (4Q208–211) attest a longer, more technical version than the Ethiopic preserves.

◇ Note. The moon as junior luminary. The terminology — "greater" sun and "smaller" moon — comes directly from Genesis 1:16, where the two great lights are subordinate to a precise hierarchy. The Astronomical Book reads Genesis 1’s concise notice as the seed of an elaborate lunar theology: the moon is junior in glory but indispensable to the calendar. The Enochic position that the sun, not the moon, governs the year underwrites the 364-day solar calendar polemic against the lunar reckoning that came to dominate later Second Temple Judaism.

◇ Note. Sevenfold lunar phases. The moon’s light is reckoned in sevenths — accumulating to fullness over fourteen "parts" (= 14 days) and then waning over fourteen more. The calculation imposes a sabbatical-numerical structure on natural phenomena: even the moon obeys the sevens that govern Israel’s sacred time. Charles flagged the lunar chapters (73–74) as containing internal inconsistencies, suggesting the compiler stitched together more than one astronomical source; nevertheless the basic seven-fold scheme is consistent and theologically motivated.

◇ Note. The hierarchy of angelic supervisors. Uriel is "leader of them all" — a ranking confirmed by 1 Enoch 20’s list, where Uriel is named first among seven archangels (the larger list, vs. the four-archangel list of ch 9). The Aramaic 4QEnastra (4Q209) preserves a rigorous chain of stewardship: stars have leaders, leaders have chiefs, chiefs report to Uriel. The cosmos is a hierarchical bureaucracy of angels, enforcing law-like motions. The image directly opposes any Hellenistic mechanistic-cosmos view: nature is not autonomous but actively governed.

◇ Note. The four extra days that make 364. The Enochic solar calendar adds four "intercalary" days to the basic 360-day schema (12 × 30) — one each at the equinoxes and solstices. The result, 364, is the polemical heart of the Astronomical Book: 364 days = exactly 52 weeks, so every date falls on the same day of the week each year; sabbaths and festivals are stable and never collide. 75:1 here is also a theological complaint: those who reject the four extra days "go wrong" — the calendar is morally weighted, not merely calendrical.

'And the leaders of the heads of the thousands, who are placed over the whole creation and over all the stars, have also to do with the four intercalary days, being inseparable from their office' — the intercalary days that complete the calendar's precision. Tewahedo Bāḥrä Ḥasab computation retains the principle of intercalary correction (the 'epact' adjustment) as a fixed angelic ordinance rather than ad-hoc fix: the calendar's accuracy is part of the cosmic priestly liturgy entrusted to the heavenly angelic leaders.

◇ Note. The 364-day solar year — sectarian centerpiece. The schema fixes festivals to permanent weekday-positions: Passover always on the same day, Pentecost always Sunday, etc. This was the calendar adopted by the Qumran community (compare the calendrical texts 4Q317–4Q330; 11QPsalms-a’s "David’s Compositions" enumerates 364 sacrifices per year). The conflict with the lunar/lunisolar 354-day reckoning of mainstream Second Temple Judaism is decisive — these are incompatible calendars producing different festival dates. VanderKam, The Origins and the Earliest History of the 364-Day Calendar (1992), is the standard treatment; the 364-day scheme is an antediluvian revelation predating Moses, hence (the argument runs) authoritative over the lunar reform.

◇ Note. The twelve wind portals. Three winds for each cardinal direction = twelve total, paralleling the twelve solar portals. The wind-cosmology is moral: of the twelve, only four bring blessing (76:4); the other eight bring drought, locusts, plague. The numerical ratio (4:8) matches the Astronomical Book’s pessimism about human reception of divine order. Compare the Babylonian wind-classification in the Erra Epic and the four-winds image in Daniel 7:2 and Zechariah 6:5 — winds are agents of divine purpose.

'And at the ends of the earth I saw twelve portals open to all the regions, from which the winds go forth and blow over the earth' — twelve wind-portals matching the twelve solar gates. Tewahedo iconographic tradition preserves the twelve-wind imagery in some monastic-church frescoes where the cardinal-and-intermediate directions are personified as angelic figures — drawing on the Astronomical Book's catalog of cosmic presences as canonical authority.

◇ Note. Moralized meteorology. Winds are not neutral phenomena but instruments of divine judgment or favor. The 4:8 ratio of beneficial-to-harmful winds anticipates apocalyptic literature’s grim arithmetic — the world’s default state is hostile, and the righteous remnant subsists by exception. Wind-judgment imagery threads through Jeremiah 4:11–12, 49:36, 51:1; Ezekiel 13:11–13; Hosea 13:15. The Astronomical Book systematizes a scattered prophetic motif into a fixed cosmic order.

◇ Note. Eden as cosmic geography. The "garden of righteousness" in the far north is Eden / Paradise — the tradition that the original garden survives at the world’s edge, accessible only to the righteous after death (compare 1 Enoch 32:3, 60:8, 4 Ezra 4:7). The far north as paradise reverses the biblical Eden’s "in the east" location (Gen 2:8), reflecting the Astronomical Book’s independent cosmography. The "garden" motif as the dwelling of the righteous after death is fully developed in chapters 22–32 (the Book of Watchers); its placement in the earth’s geography is one of 1 Enoch’s most distinctive contributions.

'And I saw seven mountains higher than all the mountains which are on the earth: and thence comes forth hoar-frost, and days, seasons, and years pass away. And I saw seven rivers on the earth larger than all the rivers' — sevenfold cosmic geography. Tewahedo monastic geography (the naming of seven holy mountains in Ethiopia, including Debre Damo, Debre Libanos, and the Lalibela complex) preserves the seven-mountains motif as Tewahedo holy-land cosmography, drawing on this Enochic vision.

◇ Note. Seven mountains, seven rivers, seven islands. The sacred number imposes itself on geography. The "seven high mountains" likely include Hermon (where the Watchers descend in 1 En 6:6), Sinai, Zion, and Ararat (compare the unnamed "seven mountains" of the 1 Enoch 18:6–8 vision; some commentators identify them with the throne complex). The seven-fold structuring threads through the Apocalypse of John (seven churches, seven seals, seven trumpets, seven bowls). The principle: holy number patterns the cosmos.

◇ Note. The moon’s distinct legislative regime. The Astronomical Book treats the moon’s motion as a separate legislative system — parallel to the sun’s but subordinate. The detail matters polemically: the lunar reckoning was authoritative for many Second Temple Jewish groups (and remains so in rabbinic Judaism), and the Enochic position that the moon possesses its own law (rather than primary calendrical authority) is the structural ground of the 364-day polemic. Charles observed that the lunar chapters’ technical detail exceeds the solar — possibly because the Aramaic source addressed the harder-to-explain irregularities, especially the moon’s roughly 11-day annual lag behind the solar year (74:11).

◇ Note. Stars by name. Direct echo of Psalm 147:4 (“He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names”). The Parables develops this into a doctrine of celestial-bodies-as-personalities: each star has a name and obeys a divine call. The motif is foundational for the later astrological angelology of Hellenistic Jewish and early Christian thought (Origen, Pseudo-Dionysius), and links 1 Enoch’s astronomical material in chs 72–82 to its theology of cosmic obedience.

◇ Note. Stars as souls. The angel reveals the interpretive key: the stars represent the names of the righteous on earth. The same identification appears in Daniel 12:3 (“those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky, and those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars forever”). The Parables sharpens the parallelism — the stars are not merely a simile for the righteous but are inscribed with their names. The motif persists into rabbinic literature and into the Christian iconography of saints crowned with stars.

◇ Note. Astral metamorphosis. The single-verse chapter records a brief vision of stars transforming into lightnings. The image draws on the interrelated Hebrew vocabulary of divine fire — baraq (lightning) and the seraphic “lightning” of Ezekiel 1. The lightning-tradition continues in the Astronomical Book’s meteorological mechanisms (chs 41, 76). Charles read this verse as a displaced fragment from a longer Astronomical sequence; Black retained it in place as a theologically significant interlude before the Second Parable.

◇ Note. Irreversible transformation. The text emphasizes that the metamorphosis is permanent: stars-become-lightnings cannot return to their original form. The motif of irreversible cosmic change foreshadows the Second Parable’s vision of the new heaven and new earth (45:4–5). Whether the verse is reading the transformation as punishment (paralleling the bound stars of 1 Enoch 18:13–16) or as glorification (stars manifesting their fullest brilliance) is interpretively open.

◇ Note. Marker of editorial structure. The text explicitly labels itself “the Second Parable” — one of the rare moments in apocalyptic literature where the editorial structure is signaled in-text. The thematic focus shifts from the righteous-versus-sinner contrast of the First Parable to the eschatological judgment by the Elect One on the throne of glory. The Second Parable is the longest of the three (chs 45–57) and contains the most concentrated Son of Man material in 1 Enoch.

◇ Note. Throne of glory. The image of the Elect One enthroned for judgment is the closest extant Jewish parallel to Matthew 25:31 (“when the Son of Man comes in his glory … he will sit on his throne of glory”) and to Matthew 19:28. The verbal echo (“throne of glory” in both 1 Enoch and the Synoptic apocalyptic discourse) is strong enough that most scholars now view the Parables as either a direct source for the Synoptic Son of Man tradition or as a parallel witness to a shared apocalyptic register.

◇ Note. Head of Days = Ancient of Days. Direct verbal allusion to Daniel 7:9 (“the Ancient of Days took his seat… his clothing was white as snow, and the hair of his head like pure wool”). The Parables interprets Daniel 7’s enthronement vision as a real heavenly council scene with two figures: the Ancient/Head of Days and “one like a son of man” approaching him. The dependency direction is clear: 1 Enoch’s Parables exegete and elaborate Daniel 7. The “son of man” of Daniel becomes a determinate heavenly figure here, preparing the ground for the Synoptic Son of Man Christology.

◇ Note. The Son of Man title. This is the first of many uses of “Son of Man” as a title in 1 Enoch (the figure recurs in 46:2–4, 48:2, 62–63, 69:26–29, 70–71). Three theses on the relationship to the Synoptic usage of Jesus are debated: (1) the Parables directly shape the Synoptic Christology (Boccaccini, Bock); (2) both draw on a common second-temple Jewish tradition no longer extant (Casey, Bauckham); (3) the flow runs from Synoptics back into the Parables via Christian redaction — a minority view hard to sustain given the manuscript history. Whichever direction, the parallel is the most theologically loaded between any OT-related apocalypse and the Christian gospels.

◇ Note. Heavenly liturgy. The angels-as-liturgists motif develops the older tradition of the divine council (Ps 82, 89) into a formal liturgical setting. The angels’ united-voice supplication “on behalf of the blood of the righteous” anticipates Revelation 6:10 (“how long, O Lord, holy and true… will you not judge and avenge our blood?”). The Parables and Revelation share the structural turn of a heavenly cry for vindication preceding eschatological judgment.

◇ Note. Books of judgment. The “books of the living” motif draws on Exodus 32:32 (Moses asking to be blotted from God’s book), Daniel 7:10 (books opened in the throne vision), and Daniel 12:1. By the time of the Parables the motif has become a fixed eschatological apparatus: opened books contain records of righteous and wicked deeds. The Christian transposition into Revelation 20:12 (“books were opened… and another book was opened, which is the book of life”) inherits the Enochic vocabulary nearly intact.

◇ Note. Naming before creation. The Son of Man’s “naming” before the Head of Days is the climactic moment of the Parables’ Christology. In Jewish-Hellenistic thought names are not mere labels — they encapsulate identity and destiny. The Son of Man’s pre-cosmic naming establishes his pre-existence, a feature that becomes constitutive of Johannine Christology (John 1:1; 17:5). The continuity between Enochic Son of Man and Johannine Logos is the single strongest argument for the Parables’ importance to New Testament theology.

◇ Note. Pre-existence and hiddenness. Two doctrines compressed into a single verse: (1) pre-existence — chosen before creation; (2) hiddenness — concealed until the eschatological revelation. Both motifs reappear in 1 Peter 1:20 (“foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times for your sake”). The Pauline and Johannine “mystery hidden for ages” (Col 1:26; Eph 3:9; Rom 16:25) shares the same conceptual structure. The Parables provides the most explicit Jewish formulation of a pre-existent and hidden Messiah.

'For wisdom is poured out like water, and glory faileth not before him for evermore' — wisdom-as-water. The verb “standing” in apocalyptic literature signals readiness for judgment or service before the divine throne (Dan 7:10 “ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him”; Rev 7:9). The Elect One’s standing posture differentiates him from the seated Lord of Spirits — he is the agent, not the principal, of judgment. This functional subordinationism — the Elect One acts on behalf of the Lord of Spirits — parallels the post-Pauline Christology of 1 Corinthians 15:24–28.

The list of spirits — wisdom, insight, understanding, might, righteousness — directly recalls Isaiah 11:2’s messianic endowment (“the spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord”). The Parables explicitly applies the Isaianic Messiah-pneumatology to the Elect One. The “seven spirits before the throne” motif of Revelation 1:4, 4:5, and 5:6 picks up this trajectory.

The motif of name-mediated salvation is Hebraically grounded (Ps 54:1 “save me, O God, by your name”) but takes a particular Second Temple form: the divine name is itself the saving instrument. Acts 4:12 (“there is no other name under heaven… by which we must be saved”) is the New Testament’s most concentrated formulation, transposed onto the name of Jesus. The Parables’ “name of the Lord of Spirits” sits at the head of this trajectory.

◇ Note. Solar nomenclature. The two names of the sun (Orjârês, Tômâs) are likely Aramaic transcriptions, possibly drawing on Ugaritic / Northwest-Semitic mythological tradition. The naming of celestial bodies distinguishes 1 Enoch’s Astronomical Book from later astronomical texts: the practice treats stars and luminaries as personalized beings deserving proper names. The four moon-names of the next verse may correspond to lunar phases (waxing, full, waning, new), although the etymologies remain uncertain.

◇ Note. Lunar fractions. The lunar accounting system — 14 parts of growing light, then 14 of waning — encodes the 28-day lunar month into a computable schema. This fourteen-fold partition has parallels in the Babylonian ziqpu-star system but with distinctive Israelite framing. The Astronomical Book’s central concern is the calendar dispute of late Second Temple Judaism: a 364-day solar year (advocated by Jubilees and the Qumran community) versus a luni-solar reckoning. The Parables-Astronomical Book material favors the solar calendar; that the chapter devotes detailed attention to lunar mechanics anyway reflects the practical need to account for both systems.

◇ Note. Resurrection of the dead. One of the clearest pre-Christian Jewish formulations of bodily resurrection: earth, Sheol, and hell each return their dead. The triple parallelism extends Daniel 12:2 (which speaks only of those “who sleep in the dust of the earth”) by adding Sheol and hell as separate custodians. Revelation 20:13 (“the sea gave up the dead that were in it, Death and Hades gave up the dead in them”) is the closest verbal parallel in the New Testament — the same triadic structure of restitution before the throne.

The three-way restitution (earth gives back bodies; Sheol gives back righteous spirits; hell gives back the wicked) anticipates the New Testament resurrection accounts. The Mäṣḥafä Hēnok is read liturgically in Tewahedo funeral rites for precisely this verse's hope.

The shared-throne motif is one of the strongest links between the Parables and the throne-Christology of Revelation 3:21 (“the one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne, as I also conquered and sat down with my Father on his throne”) and 22:1 (“the throne of God and of the Lamb”). The Parables thus provides Jewish precedent for what later Trinitarian theology will systematize as homoousios.

◇ Note. The Anointed. One of only two occurrences in the Parables of the term “Anointed” (Hebrew māšîaḥ, Greek christos) — see also 48:10. The seven metal-mountains vision is interpreted as serving the dominion of God’s “Anointed.” The use is striking because elsewhere the Parables prefers the titles Elect One, Righteous One, and Son of Man over the more direct Messiah. The dual vocabulary suggests these titles were considered functionally equivalent rather than competing — a unified messianic figure under different aspects.

◇ Note. Angels of punishment. The Parables develops a class of executioner-angels distinct from the watchers (sinners) and from the holy angels. They prepare instruments — chains, scourges — for the eschatological judgment. The motif appears in Matthew 18:34 (“the master delivered him to the jailers/torturers until he should pay”) and 25:41 (“the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels”). The conceptual structure is identical: forensic angels with prepared instruments awaiting the judgment day.

◇ Note. House of the congregation. The phrase points to an eschatological gathered community — a “house” (bayit) coextensive with the assembly of the elect. The vocabulary anticipates the New Testament image of the church as “the household of God” (Eph 2:19; 1 Tim 3:15) and Hebrews 3:6 (“we are his house”). The Qumran community used parallel architectural metaphors for itself (1QS 8:5–9). The Parables shows that the architectural-ecclesial metaphor is fully developed in Second Temple Jewish thought before any Christian appropriation.

◇ Note. The abyss. The “abyss” (abyssos, Hebrew tehom) appears in 1 Enoch as a discrete eschatological location — not Sheol (the realm of the dead) but a deeper place of condemnation reserved for the fallen Watchers and the wicked angelic powers. Revelation 9:1–11 and 20:1–3 (the binding of Satan in the abyss) inherit this Enochic vocabulary directly; Luke 8:31 (the demons begging not to be sent into the abyss) shows the same conceptual geography. The two-tiered Sheol/Abyss structure is one of 1 Enoch’s distinctive contributions to Jewish-Christian eschatology.

◇ Note. Four archangels as eschatological executioners. The same four archangels named in 40:9 reappear here as the agents of the final binding. Their placement is significant: the Parables consistently distributes eschatological functions across this archangelic college rather than concentrating them in a single figure. By contrast, Revelation 20 places the binding-of-Satan task in the hands of an unnamed angel. The four-fold distribution preserves a more collective angelology, possibly reflecting an older tradition before angelological hierarchies became more centralized.

◇ Note. Divine repentance. The Head of Days reflects on the flood — “In vain have I destroyed all who dwell on the earth” — echoing Genesis 6:6–7’s notion of divine sorrow over creation. The Parables takes the conventional flood-aftermath material and reframes it eschatologically: God promises restraint until the eschatological judgment, when the Elect One will render final decision. The same theological move appears in 2 Peter 3:9 (“the Lord is not slow concerning his promise… but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish”), where divine forbearance is reframed as eschatological deferral.

◇ Note. Judgment of Azazel. The Elect One enthroned for the climactic judgment of the chief Watcher Azazel — the figure first introduced in 1 Enoch 8 as the teacher of forbidden metallurgy and warfare. The Parables binds together its Christology (the enthroned Elect One) with the older Watcher-narrative tradition: the same heavenly judge who renders verdicts on humans also presides over the cosmic-spiritual judgment of the rebel angels. The throne-of-glory vocabulary maintains continuity with 45:3 and 62:5.

◇ Note. The chasm. The triple-noun construction (chasm, abyss, valley) intensifies the spatial metaphor for eschatological punishment. Luke 16:26’s “great chasm fixed” between Lazarus and the rich man uses the same Greek term (chasma) and presupposes the same conceptual geography. The chasm is not merely a barrier but a place — into which sinners are cast. The Parables and Luke share the assumption that postmortem judgment involves a fixed and unbridgeable spatial separation.

◇ Note. The Parthians and Medes. A historical-apocalyptic identification: the “Parthians and Medes” were the Roman Empire’s eastern adversaries throughout the first century BCE and first century CE. The Parables encodes anticipation of a Parthian invasion (the historical Parthian threat to Rome reached crisis points in 53 BCE under Crassus and again under Pacorus in 40 BCE). The eastern-invasion motif passes into Revelation 9:14 and 16:12 (the kings from the east) — the same geopolitical-apocalyptic register, transposed onto the Roman-Parthian frontier.

◇ Note. Chariot vision. The host of wagons (chariots) drawing on Ezekiel 1’s merkavah (chariot-throne) tradition — but here the chariots come to the earth from heaven rather than carrying the prophet upward. The hekhalot mystical tradition would later develop chariot-ascent into a major contemplative practice; the Parables represents an early stage where the chariot imagery is still primarily eschatological-arrival rather than ascent.

◇ Note. End of the Second Parable. The explicit structural marker closes the longest of the three parables. Such self-naming editorial signposts are rare in Jewish apocalyptic literature; their presence here helps confirm that the three-parable division of chs 38–69 is not a later editorial imposition but reflects the original compositional intent. The Third Parable (chs 58–69) follows immediately, with its own opening signpost at 58:1.

◇ Note. The Third Parable opens. The third and final Parable focuses on the lot of the righteous, the final judgment of the kings and the mighty, and culminates in the Noachic interpolations (chs 65–69) and Enoch’s translation/identification with the Son of Man (chs 70–71). The opening blessing-form (“Blessed are ye, ye righteous and elect”) parallels the macarisms of Matthew 5:3–11 and Luke 6:20–22 — both compositions deploy beatitudes as eschatological declarations of inverted fortune.

◇ Note. Light of the sun. The exact image appears in Matthew 13:43: “Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father.” Both texts draw on Daniel 12:3 (“those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky”), but the specifically solar comparison is shared between 1 Enoch and Matthew, suggesting a closer Enochic background for the Synoptic image than the Danielic intertext alone would predict. The motif of luminosity as glorification connects to the transfiguration narratives (Matt 17:2; 2 Cor 3:18).

◇ Note. Meteorological ethics. Lightning and weather are described as carrying out judgment — they “lighten for blessing or curse.” The conception treats meteorological phenomena as moral agents under angelic supervision (paralleling chs 41 and 76). The Christian reception of this tradition is complex: while modern theology generally rejects the idea of natural events as direct divine moral judgments, the conception remained influential in early and medieval Christian thought (Augustine’s discussion of the saints and weather in City of God; the meteorological saints of medieval folk religion).

◇ Note. Voice from heaven. Thunder as the divine voice is biblically rooted (Ps 29; Job 37:2–5; Exod 19:16). The Parables systematizes the tradition: thunder communicates judgment and blessing as audible divine speech. The motif of the thunderous voice from heaven persists into the New Testament (Mark 1:11 at Jesus’ baptism; Mark 9:7 at the transfiguration; John 12:28–29 where the crowd interprets the heavenly voice as thunder). The Parables provides background for understanding why a heavenly voice would be apprehended as thunder: the conceptual identification was already standard.

◇ Note. Noachic chronological frame. The precise date — year 500, month 7, day 14 — together with the Ethiopic textual variant placing the vision “in the life of Noah” rather than Enoch (the bracketed reading) marks ch 60 as one of the chapters incorporated from a hypothetical lost “Book of Noah.” Genesis 5:32 records Noah’s age at 500 when he begot his sons, and Genesis 7:11 dates the flood to the seventeenth day of the second month. The chronological apparatus signals a Noachic source, even though the redactor framed the material as Enochic.

◇ Note. Behemoth and Leviathan parted. The chapter introduces Behemoth and Leviathan as paired primordial monsters (Behemoth on the dry land east of Eden; Leviathan in the abyss of the sea). The pairing is rooted in Job 40:15–41:34, but the Parables systematizes the tradition: the two are eschatologically reserved as a banquet for the righteous in the messianic age. The motif passes into 4 Ezra 6:49–52 and 2 Baruch 29:4 with the same imagery, and is preserved in rabbinic literature (b. Bava Batra 74b–75a) and possibly echoed in Revelation 13’s two beasts (from sea and earth).

◇ Note. Cords for measuring. The motif of angelic measurement of the elect parallels Zechariah 2:1–5 (the man with the measuring line for Jerusalem) and Revelation 11:1–2 (John given a measuring rod for the temple). The act of measurement signifies divine election and protection: what is measured is preserved. The Parables extends the tradition: the measures are not for a city or temple but for the bodies of the resurrected righteous, including those “destroyed by the desert” or “devoured by the beasts” — i.e., the remains gathered for restoration.

◇ Note. Universal resurrection. The catalogue of the lost — those killed in the desert, eaten by beasts, drowned in the sea — establishes the doctrine of universal bodily resurrection: even those whose bodies are dispersed beyond ordinary recovery will be restored. The same exhaustive cataloguing appears in Revelation 20:13. The motif addresses a real ancient concern (martyrdom, exile, sea voyages, military death without burial) and asserts that material dispersion is no obstacle to divine restoration.

◇ Note. Son of Man on the throne of glory — third major occurrence. After the introduction in 46:1–4 and the naming-before-creation of 48:2, this is the climactic enthronement scene. The “throne of his glory” language is the closest verbal antecedent to Matthew 25:31 (“when the Son of Man comes in his glory … he will sit on his throne of glory”). The visual structure — kings and the mighty seeing him sit, and being terrified — directly anticipates the eschatological-judgment scenes of the Synoptic Son of Man material.

◇ Note. Hidden from the beginning. The doctrine of pre-existence and concealment, already stated at 48:6, is reasserted here at the climactic moment of the Son of Man’s revelation. “Hidden” is a precise theological term: not absent but reserved, present but undisclosed. The Pauline “mystery hidden for ages” (Rom 16:25; Col 1:26; Eph 3:9) and the Johannine pre-existent Logos (John 1:1–14) inherit this conceptual structure. The Parables thus provides what is arguably the closest pre-Christian Jewish parallel to the New Testament’s pre-existent-and-revealed Christology.

◇ Note. Fourfold lordship doxology. The condemned kings recite a four-titled doxology: Lord of Spirits, Lord of kings, Lord of the mighty, Lord of glory. The structure parallels the eschatological doxology of Revelation 19:16 (“King of kings and Lord of lords”) and 1 Timothy 6:15. The condemned-confess-the-throne motif is a recurrent element of apocalyptic theology: the very ones judged are forced into liturgical recognition of the judge — anticipating Philippians 2:10–11’s universal confession of Jesus’ lordship even from the unwilling.

◇ Note. The anguish of the damned. The condemned kings’ lament — desiring rest but finding none, surrounded by darkness — parallels Luke 16:24 (the rich man begging for water) and Revelation 14:11 (“the smoke of their torment … they have no rest, day or night”). The “no rest” formulation in particular is shared verbatim between 1 Enoch and Revelation. The Parables thus supplies one of the conceptual antecedents to the New Testament’s depiction of postmortem suffering not as fire alone but as restless cognitive anguish.

◇ Note. Hidden forms. The brief two-verse chapter introduces the descended Watchers as “hidden forms” — beings whose forbidden revelations are themselves “hidden things” improperly disclosed. The Parables-Noachic interpolation begins here. The vocabulary of hiddenness (already developed for the pre-existent Son of Man in 48:6 and 62:7) is now applied negatively: hidden disclosures by fallen angels stand in contrast to hidden disclosures by the Lord of Spirits.

◇ Note. Forbidden revelation. The fallen angels’ offense is not the descent itself but the disclosure: they revealed what was hidden. The motif distinguishes legitimate revelation (granted by the Lord of Spirits to chosen seers like Enoch) from illegitimate revelation (forced disclosure by Watchers to humanity). The category is significant for understanding Second Temple epistemology: knowledge has provenance, and provenance determines moral status. The same logic underlies Paul’s distinction between “the wisdom of this age” (1 Cor 2:6) and “the wisdom of God in a mystery” (1 Cor 2:7).

◇ Note. Noah’s vision begins. Chs 65–67 form a Noachic interpolation — the speaker shifts to Noah, who narrates his visit to Enoch (his great-grandfather) on the eve of the flood. Charles’ edition carefully preserved these source-shifts. The Noachic tradition envisioned in these chapters continues into Jubilees 7–10 and into the Aramaic Genesis Apocryphon from Qumran (1QapGen ar). All three witness to a now-lost extended Noah-narrative tradition that circulated alongside but distinctly from Genesis 6–9.

◇ Note. Catalogue of forbidden knowledge. The list — secrets, violence, sorcery, witchcraft, metallurgy — recapitulates and expands the Watchers’ offense from chs 6–8. The cumulative catalogue functions as theological etiology: humanity’s pre-flood corruption is rooted in this illegitimate knowledge transfer. The pattern of categorizing forbidden knowledge persists into the Christian canon (Galatians 5:19–21’s “works of the flesh” catalogue; Revelation 21:8 and 22:15) and into rabbinic discussions of forbidden teachings.

◇ Note. Angels of the waters. A specialized class of angelic functionaries oversees the cosmic waters — the same conception as the angels of lightning, thunder, and the calendar. The Parables systematizes Hebrew Bible glimpses (e.g., Ps 104:4) into a developed angelology: every cosmic element has its angelic supervisor. The motif persists into Revelation 16:5 (“the angel of the waters”) and into the medieval Christian tradition of the four elemental angels, then into the Kabbalistic sarim (princes) over each element.

◇ Note. Restraining the waters. The flood is depicted as requiring divine restraint of the angels who would naturally release the cosmic waters — without explicit command, the waters would burst forth. The conception assumes that creation’s stability is actively maintained, not merely set in motion at creation. This active-providence cosmology underlies later patristic and rabbinic theology of creatio continua (continuous creation), distinct from a deistic-mechanical conception.

◇ Note. Noah’s lot. The phrase “a lot without blame” parallels Genesis 6:9’s description of Noah as “righteous, blameless in his generation.” But the Parables uses lot-language (klēros): Noah’s name has come up before God by lot, signaling election rather than mere virtue. The lot-vocabulary persists into the New Testament, especially in Acts 1:17 (Judas “was numbered among us and received his portion”) and 1 Peter 5:3 (“not lording it over those allotted to your charge”) — the same election-by-lot conception.

◇ Note. Imprisoned angels. The doctrine that the Watchers are imprisoned awaiting eschatological judgment — present in 1 Enoch 10 already — is reasserted here in Noah’s vision. 2 Peter 2:4 (“God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell and committed them to chains of gloomy darkness to be kept until the judgment”) and Jude 6 (“the angels who did not stay within their own position of authority … he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day”) explicitly draw on this Enochic doctrine. Both Petrine and Jude texts presuppose familiarity with 1 Enoch in their original audience.

◇ Note. Angelic dialogue. A rare moment of inter-angelic conversation: Michael and Raphael discuss the severity of the eschatological judgment. The trope of named angelic dialogue is a development beyond the Hebrew Bible’s largely silent or single-voiced angels (Gabriel speaks in Daniel 8–9; Michael is named but does not speak in Daniel 10–12). The Parables sets a precedent for the elaborated angelic dialogues of later apocalyptic and hekhalot literature — and indirectly for the ranks of Cherubim and Seraphim singing antiphonally in early Christian liturgy.

◇ Note. Neither angel nor man. The fallen angels (and their hybrid offspring) are excluded from both classes — neither participating in the eternal lot of the angels nor receiving the resurrection-portion of redeemed humanity. The Parables thus develops a tripartite eschatological taxonomy: redeemed humans, holy angels, and irretrievable fallen powers. Hebrews 2:16 (“surely it is not angels he helps, but the offspring of Abraham”) reflects the same exclusion: redemption is for humans, with the angelic destiny structurally distinct.

◇ Note. Catalogue of the Watchers. The list of 21 named Watchers parallels (with variations) the catalogue in 1 Enoch 6:7. Samjâzâ (also Shemihazah) heads the list as chief of the descent. Many of the names are reconstructible to Hebrew/Aramaic etymologies: Kôkabêl (“star of God”), Barâqêl (“lightning of God”), Azâzêl (the chief sin-bearer of the day). Onomastic catalogues like this had liturgical-magical functions in some Second Temple circles and contributed to the later Jewish-Christian angelological tradition through the Sefer Raziel and the Christian apocryphal apocalypses.

◇ Note. Gadreel — Eve’s seducer. Distinct from Samjâzâ (the descent-leader) and Azâzêl (the metallurgy-teacher), Gâdreêl is identified as the angel who specifically led Eve astray and taught humanity the weapons of war. This tripartite distribution of guilt — descent, metallurgy, sexual seduction — is a development beyond the simpler Genesis 3 narrative of the serpent. The Parables thus stands at the developmental hinge between the Hebrew Bible’s nameless serpent and the later Jewish/Christian identification of the Edenic tempter with Satan (Wisdom 2:24; Rev 12:9).

◇ Note. Enoch’s translation — distinct from the Son of Man. The verse describes Enoch being raised to the Son of Man — implying two distinct figures at this point. The careful phrasing matters: Enoch’s translation does not (yet) identify him with the Son of Man, who remains the heavenly figure to whom Enoch is brought. The identification will emerge only in ch 71. The narrative structure resembles a coronation sequence: Enoch is presented, then formally identified.

◇ Note. Removal from earth. The phrase echoes Genesis 5:24 (“Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him”) and Hebrews 11:5 (“by faith Enoch was taken up so that he should not see death, and he was not found, because God had taken him”). The Parables inherits the cryptic Genesis statement and elaborates it: Enoch is removed not by death but by translation — and the place of his removal is between the north and the west, where the angels measure the dwelling of the elect.

◇ Note. Ascent vision. The chapter opens the climactic ascent — Enoch’s spirit translated into the heaven of heavens, encountering crystals and living fire. The vision-pattern is the foundational template for the later hekhalot mystical tradition (the “Greater Hekhalot” and “Lesser Hekhalot” texts, the Shi’ur Qomah, etc.) and provides Christian apocalyptic literature with its model for visionary ascent (2 Cor 12:2–4 “caught up to the third heaven… whether in the body or out of the body I do not know”).

◇ Note. Enoch identified as the Son of Man.
