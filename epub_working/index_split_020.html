◇ Note. The opening of 1 Enoch. Modern scholarship dates the Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36) to the 3rd century BCE — making it the oldest part of 1 Enoch and one of the oldest Jewish apocalyptic texts. The Tewahedo reading takes this not as mythological folklore but as actual cosmic-historical rebellion: the disordering of the angelic order is the proximate cause of the disordering of the human order that necessitates the Flood. This is the Tewahedo canonical anchor that distinguishes its angelology from those of other Christian traditions.

◇ Note. 1 Enoch 6 expands Genesis 6:1–4. Where Genesis gives only two enigmatic verses about ‘sons of God’ taking human wives, 1 Enoch tells the full story — 200 angels descending, swearing a mutual oath, taking wives, fathering giants. ◇ Note. Mount Hermon as the boundary, and the Jared word-play. Hermon stands at the northernmost edge of biblical Israel. In ancient Near Eastern geography it was a divine mountain, the home of Baal. The text says the Watchers descended ‘in the days of Jared’ — a Hebrew word-play, since Jared (יֶרֶד, Yered) means ‘he descended.’ The author may also be punning on Hebrew cherem (devoted to destruction, banned), turning Hermon into ‘the mountain of the ban.’

◇ Note. The giants of 1 Enoch. Greek gigantes, Aramaic nephilin/gibborin. The text reads as a theological explanation for what Genesis only hints at: the pre-Flood corruption was not merely human sin but the cosmos itself unraveling under hybrid contamination. The Tewahedo reading takes this as the prototype of every subsequent technology-corrupted-by-fallen-knowledge: the technologies themselves are not evil, but their introduction by rebel angels (rather than by the proper covenantal tradition) makes them weapons of disorder rather than tools of stewardship. This is the textual root of the Tewahedo monastic ascetic suspicion of certain technological luxuries.

◇ Note. Cosmetics as moral corruption. The list of Azazel’s teachings frames cosmetics, eye-paint, and ornamentation as corrupting knowledge alongside warfare. Some feminist scholars have noted the gendered weight of this list: women’s adornment is treated as equivalent to men’s warfare in degree of corruption.

◇ Note. The forbidden curriculum. 1 Enoch 8:3 catalogues different Watchers as teachers of different forbidden disciplines: astrology, divination, augury. This is one of our earliest extensive lists of ‘magical’ knowledge from Second Temple Judaism. The implicit theology: human technology and learning are not neutral — they have origins, and some of those origins are corrupting. ◇ Note. The four (or seven) archangels. 1 Enoch is one of the earliest texts to name specific archangels. The four named here — Michael, Uriel, Raphael, Gabriel — will become canonical in later Jewish and Christian tradition. 1 Enoch 20 will list seven; the New Testament names only Michael and Gabriel, with Raphael appearing in the Book of Tobit. The named-angel theology of 1 Enoch is foundational for all subsequent angelology.

The Flood does not just punish sin; it cleanses contamination. This frames the Flood narrative differently from Genesis: the problem isn’t merely human wickedness, it is a cosmic infection that requires hydraulic surgery.

◇ Note. This delayed-resolution apocalyptic logic — ‘wait for it; God will act; the universe will be unmade and remade’ — is the theological grammar 1 Enoch contributed to Judaism, and through Judaism to Christianity and Islam. ◇ Note. Enoch’s cosmic tour begins. Chapters 17–19 (and 20–36) narrate Enoch being taken by angels to see hidden parts of the cosmos: the storehouses of winds, the mountain of God, the prison of fallen stars, the place of judgment. ✧ Textual. 1 Enoch 1:9 quoted in Jude 14–15. The opening prophecy here — “Behold, He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones” — is quoted nearly verbatim by Jude in the New Testament (Jude 14–15), where it is attributed explicitly to “Enoch the seventh from Adam.” Jude’s Greek differs in small ways from both the Akhmim Greek of Codex Panopolitanus and the Geʻez of this Bible — he may be quoting a Greek translation no longer extant or paraphrasing from memory. This citation is the central evidence that 1 Enoch was authoritative Scripture for at least some first-century Jewish-Christian communities; it remains canonical only in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church today.

◇ Note. Enoch as visionary, not just patriarch. Genesis 5:24 says only that “Enoch walked with God, and he was not.” 1 Enoch fills out the silence with a fully developed visionary tradition. The phrase “whose eyes were opened” (ʼīrā ḥazwōā in Geʻez) corresponds in the Greek (Akhmim) fragment to anōigménon (ἀνεῦγμένον) and aligns with later Jewish and Christian apocalyptic seer-vocabulary (cf. ◇ Note. The luminaries’ obedience. The opening of the Book of Watchers’ second discourse appeals to natural-theological observation: the heavenly luminaries (sun, moon, stars) keep their appointed orbits without deviation, and Enoch invites the reader to observe this orderly obedience as silent rebuke to human disorder. The argument has deep roots in Israelite wisdom theology (cf. Ps 19:1–6, the heavens declaring God’s glory; Jer 31:35–36, the cosmic order as covenant pledge) but 1 Enoch develops it polemically: where the human community has disordered its affairs through the Watchers’ corruption, the cosmos itself remains righteous. The argument from cosmic regularity becomes one of the structural premises of apocalyptic literature and reappears in NT cosmology (Col 1:16–17, "in him all things hold together"). The verse establishes 1 Enoch’s creation-theology framework: cosmic obedience is the moral standard against which human disobedience is measured.

◇ Note. The seasonal argument. The seasonal alternation — summer’s heat, winter’s rain and dew — is presented as further visible evidence of created order. The argument continues the chapter’s pedagogical method: Enoch directs the reader’s attention to recurring natural phenomena that even the most cursory observation confirms, and frames them as theological data. The Aramaic original (preserved at Qumran in 4QEna) expands the seasonal catalog with detail about clouds, rain, and dew — natural phenomena were a major focus of Second Temple cosmological speculation, and this chapter participates in a wider literature of natural-philosophical reflection (cf. Job 38–41, Sirach 43, Wisd 7:17–22). The pattern of seasonal regularity becomes especially important for the Astronomical Book (1 Enoch 72–82), which catalogs the precise calendrical mechanics by which the sun, moon, and stars produce these visible patterns.

◇ Note. The fourteen evergreens. The reference to "fourteen trees which do not lose their foliage" is one of 1 Enoch’s small naturalistic-historical details: the Aramaic original at Qumran (4QEna) preserves a similar count, and the number reflects the recognized evergreens of the Mediterranean-Levantine flora — cedar, pine, cypress, oak species, olive, laurel, and others. The chapter participates in the wider Second Temple genre of cosmic catalog-literature (cf. Sirach 24:13–17, the wisdom-tree catalog; the Treatise of Shem and other later astrological-botanical works). The principle the chapter encodes is theological-pedagogical: even the seasonal cycle has within it items of permanence — evergreens — that resist the general pattern, and this resistance is itself part of the divine design. The chapter is brief but dense with naturalistic specificity.

◇ Note. The trees as moral metaphor. The chapter’s framing has long been read as moral as well as descriptive: the contrast between trees that lose their leaves and those that retain them prefigures the contrast between humans whose lives "wither" through sin and those who remain steadfast (cf. Ps 1:3, the righteous as a tree planted by streams; Jer 17:8, the same image; later reused at Matt 7:17–20, "by their fruits you shall know them"). 1 Enoch’s wisdom-pedagogy throughout chs 2–5 trains the reader to read natural phenomena as moral analogues. The compositional technique — beginning with cosmic observation and moving toward human moral application — becomes one of the standard rhetorical patterns of apocalyptic exhortation, recurring later in 1 Enoch itself (cf. ch 5’s climactic moral application: "ye have not been steadfast"). The brief chapter is part of a tightly structured argument.

◇ Note. Solar regularity. The chapter’s single verse focuses on the visible regularity of the sun: in summer the sun rises high, the heat is intense, and the earth seeks shade. The phenomenon is so taken for granted that we miss its rhetorical function — the sun’s annual altitude variation is one of the most precisely repeating phenomena in human experience, observable without instruments, used by every Mediterranean culture as the basis for time-keeping and agriculture. The chapter’s placement before the Astronomical Book (chs 72–82) is structural: this brief mention establishes solar observation as theologically significant data, which the later book will systematize in detail. Babylonian and Egyptian astronomical literature of the period (the MUL.APIN tablets, the Egyptian decanal lists) provided the technical infrastructure 1 Enoch’s astronomical theology builds on, and this chapter is the simplest invocation of solar phenomena before the technical material.

◇ Note. The seeker of shade. The detail that humans "seek shade and shelter by reason of the heat of the sun" is Mediterranean lived experience encoded in cosmological reflection: the sun’s power is felt directly on the body, requiring physical accommodation. The image will recur throughout apocalyptic literature as theological figure for divine intensity (cf. Rev 7:16, "they shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more, neither shall the sun strike them"; Mal 4:1–2, the sun-of-righteousness in the day of judgment). The seeker-of-shade image also captures something of 1 Enoch’s wisdom pedagogy: the natural phenomenon obviously demands accommodation; how much more does the divine reality? The chapter’s brevity is deceptive — its single verse compresses a Mediterranean climatological observation into theological argument with surprising economy. Later Jewish liturgy and Christian preaching alike will return to the sun-and-shade image repeatedly.

◇ Note. The recapitulation. The chapter rounds off the four-chapter argument from natural phenomena (chs 2–5): cosmic luminaries, seasons, evergreens, summer sun — and now the productive cycle of trees in spring, leafing and fruiting. The recapitulation prepares the moral application that follows immediately (5:4, "ye have not been steadfast"). The structure is rhetorically calibrated: pile up evidence of cosmic obedience first, then turn to human moral failure. The same pattern of nature-evidence-followed-by-moral-application recurs throughout wisdom literature (Prov 6:6–11, the ant; Prov 30:24–28, the four small wise things) and informs the broader Second Temple wisdom-pedagogical tradition. 1 Enoch’s deployment is unusually expansive — four chapters of cosmic evidence before the application — and the expansion gives the moral charge its considerable weight.

◇ Note. The moral indictment. The chapter’s climactic accusation against the unrighteous — "ye have not been steadfast, nor done the commandments of the Lord, but have turned away and spoken proud and hard words with your impure mouths against His greatness" — is the entire prior natural-theological argument applied. The cosmic luminaries were steadfast; the seasons were steadfast; humans were not. The vocabulary is theologically precise: the Aramaic ša‘adin ("steadfast") and Greek emeinate resonate with the broader Second Temple ethics of steadfastness as core covenantal virtue (cf. James 1:12, "blessed is the man who endures temptation"; the recurring NT theme of hypomonē, "endurance"). The verse functions as a hinge in 1 Enoch’s opening: from cosmic observation (chs 2–5) the argument now turns to the cosmic violation that has produced the present disorder — the Watchers’ rebellion (chs 6–11). The moral diagnosis is delivered before the historical-mythological cause is narrated.

◇ Note. The treasuries of blessing. The image of heavenly "store chambers" or "treasuries" containing rain, snow, blessing, and judgment is one of 1 Enoch’s recurring cosmological structures (cf. 18:1, "treasuries of all the winds"; 41:3–7, treasuries of stars, sun, and moon; 60:11–22, more elaborate cosmological geography). The image draws on a wider ANE conception — the heavens as architecturally divided spaces in which divine resources are stored and from which they are released according to schedule and purpose — preserved in OT idiom (Deut 28:12, "Yahweh shall open to you his good treasure, the heavens"; Ps 33:7, the deep gathered into storehouses; Job 38:22, "have you entered the treasuries of the snow?"). The chapter’s placement after the catastrophic giants-and-watchers narrative (chs 6–10) is structural: God now reverses the calamity, opening the blessings that the corruption had sealed.

◇ Note. The eschatological pairing. The pairing of "truth and peace" (Aramaic qushta wa-shelama) becomes one of Second Temple Jewish literature’s standard eschatological dyads (cf. Ps 85:10, "mercy and truth have met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other"; Zech 8:16, "speak the truth… execute true judgment and peace"). The two terms become so paired in apocalyptic and prophetic vocabulary that they often function as a single eschatological condition. The chapter’s closing — "throughout all the generations of men" — extends the eschatological vision into permanent human-cosmic order, contrasting with the chaos the Watchers introduced. The Christian liturgical formula "the peace of God which passes all understanding" (Phil 4:7) draws on this same Second Temple matrix. The verse’s prophetic-eschatological force is considerable: it asserts that the corruption diagnosed in chs 6–10 will not be the last word.

◇ Note. The genesis of evil spirits. The chapter articulates 1 Enoch’s influential theology of the origin of demons: when the Watcher-giants died in the flood (recounted at chs 7–10), their disembodied spirits did not enter Sheol (the destination of human souls) but remained on earth as malevolent forces, tormenting humanity until the final judgment. The doctrine becomes one of Second Temple Judaism’s most theologically generative innovations and shapes the demonology of the NT directly: the assumption that "demons" (Greek daimonia) are a specific kind of malevolent spiritual entity distinct from fallen angels descends from this 1 Enoch tradition. Jude 6 and 2 Pet 2:4 ("angels who sinned… reserved in chains of darkness") explicitly draw on the 1 Enoch Watchers tradition. Mark’s Gospel’s extensive demoniac narratives (Mark 1:23–27, 5:1–20, 9:14–29) operate within this conceptual framework. The chapter is one of the most theologically influential single texts in the Second Temple corpus.

Of the demons (disembodied giants): 'From the day of the slaughter and destruction and death of the giants, from the souls of whose flesh the spirits, having gone forth, shall destroy without incurring judgement' — the post-Flood era of demonic predation. The demons act 'without incurring judgement' because they exist in a category outside the original divine ordering: humans face judgment under the Adamic-Noahic-Mosaic covenant; angels face judgment for their own rebellion; demons (as a third category, post-Flood) operate in an interim awaiting final eschatological judgment. The Tewahedo exorcistic liturgical tradition draws explicit pneumatological framework from this verse.

◇ Note. The Watchers’ unauthorized teaching. The verse encodes one of 1 Enoch’s most pointed theological criticisms of the Watcher angels: they revealed to humans "mysteries" (heavenly secrets) that they themselves only partially knew, and which were not theirs to disclose. The catalog of Watchers’ teaching at 8:1–3 — metallurgy, weaponry, cosmetics, astrology, sorcery — is presented as illegitimate revelation, knowledge that should have remained sealed until proper time and proper authorization. The principle the chapter encodes is theologically significant: revelation is not just content but is also bound by authorization — wrong knowledge in the wrong hands at the wrong time is corruption rather than enlightenment. The same principle governs the prophetic-apocalyptic tradition broadly: the seer (Daniel, Enoch, John of Patmos) receives mysteries by divinely sanctioned channel, where the rebellious angels in 1 Enoch did not. The verse’s critique of unauthorized revelation remains theologically distinctive.

◇ Note. Enoch’s cosmic tour. Chapter 18 begins the famous cosmic-geography section of the Book of Watchers: Enoch is taken on a guided tour of the structures of the cosmos — wind-treasuries, the foundations of the earth, mountains pillars-of-the-earth, star-treasuries, the abyss, the prison of the rebel angels. The genre is the apocalyptic otherworldly journey, of which 1 Enoch’s Book of Watchers is the earliest extended Jewish example and one of the most influential in subsequent religious literature. The same form recurs in the Greek Apocalypse of Baruch, the Apocalypse of Paul, the medieval Christian visions of heaven and hell, and reaches its consummate medieval form in Dante’s Divine Comedy. The verse opens this tradition: Enoch as cosmic surveyor, describing what he sees with a precision that registers the theological claim that creation itself has structure that revelation discloses.

◇ Note. The seven fallen stars. The "seven stars like great burning mountains" represent fallen luminary-angels — the chapter’s vision of beings that violated their appointed orbits and therefore, like the Watchers, await final judgment. The image deeply influences NT apocalyptic literature: Rev 12:4 ("his tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven, and cast them to the earth") and Rev 8:10–11 (the burning star Wormwood) draw directly on this 1 Enoch tradition of stars as personal-angelic beings susceptible to fall. The conceptual framework — that visible heavenly bodies are also or correspond to spiritual entities — is widespread in the ancient Near Eastern and Greco-Roman world but receives its specifically apocalyptic-Jewish elaboration here. The verse is one of the foundational texts of the entire later "fallen star" theological tradition that runs through Christian eschatological literature.

◇ Note. The Watchers’ corruption. The verse confirms what chs 6–10 narrated: angels who had united with human women had transgressed not only by the union itself but by introducing demonic-corrupt teaching to humanity. Their spirits, taking many forms, continue to defile humanity (presumably by leading sacrifice to demons, as the verse continues, until the day of great judgment). The chapter’s assumption is significant: humans’ continuing religious failure (sacrifice to demons rather than to God) is partly attributable to this antecedent angelic corruption — humanity inherits a corrupted religious environment that the Watchers’ sin established. The theological principle compresses what later Christian theology will develop as "structural sin" or "fallen world": present moral evil exists within an antecedent cosmic catastrophe, and ordinary human moral choice happens within an already-disordered context. The 1 Enoch Watchers tradition becomes one of the major Second Temple sources for this anthropology.

Uriel explains: 'Here shall stand the angels who have connected themselves with women, and their spirits assuming many different forms are defiling mankind and shall lead them astray into sacrificing to demons as gods' — the post-Flood human idolatry derived from demon-led seduction. ◇ Note. Enoch’s unique vision. The chapter ends with a striking authentication-claim: Enoch alone has seen the cosmic structures, the prison of the angels, and the ends of all things. The claim functions both inside the narrative (establishing Enoch as authoritative seer) and outside it (establishing the Book of Watchers as preserving uniquely authoritative cosmological revelation). The ground for Enoch’s privileged status is Gen 5:24 ("Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him"), the laconic biblical statement, which Second Temple tradition expanded into Enoch as ascended visionary granted access to divine secrets. The verse encodes the tradition’s self-understanding: the Book of Watchers is not human speculation about cosmic geography but Enochic revelation, with the seer’s special access certified by Genesis. The principle of authorization-by-special-figure runs through the Second Temple apocalyptic genre — Daniel, Ezra, Baruch all share this dynamic. Enoch is foundational among them.

◇ Note. The seven archangels. The chapter lists seven named archangels: Uriel, Raphael, Raguel, Michael, Saraqael, Gabriel, and Remiel — the earliest extant Jewish list of named archangels with assigned cosmic functions. The list shapes subsequent angelological tradition decisively: Tobit 12:15 names Raphael as one of the seven; the NT names Michael (Jude 9, Rev 12:7) and Gabriel (Luke 1:19, 26) drawing on this tradition; later Jewish mysticism (Sefer ha-Razim, Hekhalot literature) develops the seven-archangels schema extensively. The number seven matches both Babylonian astrological angelology (the seven planetary deities of Babylonian and later Hellenistic astral religion) and biblical-cosmic patterning (seven days of creation, seven branches of the menorah). The chapter is one of the foundational texts of all later Jewish-Christian-Islamic angelology, and its seven-archangel schema becomes structurally definitive for medieval and modern religious imagination.

◇ Note. Gabriel’s function. Gabriel’s 1 Enoch portfolio — "over Paradise and the serpents and the Cherubim" — combines positive and negative cosmic responsibilities: the guardianship of Paradise (the Garden of Eden) and supervision of both the cherubim (Yahweh’s throne-bearers) and the serpents (presumably demonic serpentine beings from the broader Watchers cosmology). Enoch’s message to the fallen Watchers includes a definitive theological pronouncement: their offense is past forgiveness, with no peace and no atonement available.
