Notes

⌘ Hebrew. Bereshit (בְּרֵאשִׁית) — ‘In the beginning’. The Hebrew opening word, from which the book takes its Jewish name. Grammatically ambiguous: traditional reading takes it as an absolute (‘In the beginning, God created…’), but Rashi and several modern scholars read it as construct (‘When God began to create…’). The reading affects whether Genesis describes creation ex nihilo or God shaping a pre-existing chaos.

◇ Note Ancient Near Eastern context. Gen 1 shares structural features with the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Egyptian Memphite Theology — a divine speech-act creation, separation of waters, ordered emergence of cosmos — yet differs sharply: no theogony, no combat with chaos-monsters, a single creator, and human dignity rooted in the image-of-God rather than menial labor for the gods. The polemical contrast is part of Genesis' theological argument.

◇ Note Book summary (pedagogical). Genesis traces the world from creation (1-11: universal scope) through the patriarchs (12-50: family of promise), establishing key theological themes — covenant, blessing, election, and the persistent human tendency toward sin and divine response. It functions as the foundational narrative for the rest of the Pentateuch and the canon as a whole.

◇ Note Harmonization: Gen 1 vs Gen 2 creation accounts. Gen 1:1-2:3 and Gen 2:4-25 differ in style, sequence, and divine name. Traditional readings treat them as complementary (chapter 1 = cosmic overview, chapter 2 = anthropic close-up), with the apparent ordering tension in 2:19 resolved by reading the verb as pluperfect ("had formed"). Critical readings posit two source documents (P and J). Both views can coexist without denying scriptural authority.

⌘ Hebrew. Tohu va-vohu (תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ) — ‘formless and empty.’ A rhyming Hebrew phrase suggesting primal chaos or unproductive wasteland. The phrase recurs in Jeremiah 4:23 and Isaiah 34:11 as a portrait of judgment-induced desolation. Some scholars (Sailhamer, Walton) argue the phrase describes uninhabited wilderness rather than absolute chaos; others (Westermann, Wenham) read it as cosmic disorder awaiting divine ordering.

⌘ Hebrew. Tehom (תְּהוֹם) — ‘the deep.’ Cognate with Akkadian Tiamat, the chaos-monster goddess defeated in the Babylonian creation epic Enuma Elish. The choice of word likely positions Genesis 1 in dialogue with that older Mesopotamian myth: where Marduk defeats Tiamat in combat, Israel’s God simply organizes the deep without struggle.

◇ Note. Light before luminaries. Light is created on day 1, but the sun, moon, and stars not until day 4. The standard scholarly explanation is that Genesis 1 is structured as poetic correspondence (days 1–3 form domains, days 4–6 fill them), not as a strict scientific sequence. Augustine’s Literal Commentary on Genesis already noted this structural reading in the 5th century.

◇ Note. The plural ‘us’ in 1:26. Scholarly readings include: (1) plural of majesty, like ‘Elohim’; (2) the divine council, where God addresses the angels; (3) a self-deliberation. Christian tradition has often read this as proto-Trinitarian, but most modern Hebrew Bible scholars consider that anachronistic for the original context.

⌘ Hebrew. Tselem (צֶלֶם) and demut (דְּמוּת) — ‘image’ and ‘likeness.’ Tselem elsewhere refers to physical images (idols, statues); demut suggests resemblance more abstractly. In Ancient Near Eastern royal ideology, the king alone bore the image of the god. Genesis democratizes this: every human being bears the divine image. This is one of the most theologically consequential phrases in the Hebrew Bible.

⌘ Hebrew. Yahweh / Adam / Adamah word-play. Hebrew: vayyitser YHWH Elohim et ha-adam afar min ha-adamah. Adam (man) is formed from adamah (ground) — an earthling from earth. The word-play is untranslatable but shapes the whole chapter: humans are dust-creatures animated by divine breath.

◇ Note. Two trees, or one? Genesis 2:9 names the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; Genesis 3 only mentions the second by name during the temptation. Some scholars (Westermann) suggest the original story had only one tree, with the tree of life inserted by a later editor. The narrative as it stands creates the puzzle of Genesis 3:22–24, where the tree of life is suddenly the focus of God’s concern.

◇ Note. ‘Knowledge of good and evil’ — what is it? Mainstream readings: (1) moral consciousness — discerning right from wrong; (2) sexual maturity — the phrase used elsewhere of coming of age (Deut 1:39); (3) total knowledge as a Hebrew merism (‘everything from A to Z’); (4) autonomous moral judgment, claiming a divine prerogative. Most modern scholars favor #4.

◇ Note. The serpent. Genesis 3 calls it simply nahash (snake), not Satan. The identification of the serpent with Satan is a later development first explicit in the intertestamental literature (Wisdom of Solomon 2:24) and the New Testament (Revelation 12:9). In the original Hebrew narrative, the serpent is a clever talking creature, more cunning than other animals — not yet the cosmic adversary of later tradition.

◇ Note. The serpent’s temptation. Hebrew: k’Elohim — ‘like God’ or ‘like the gods.’ The temptation is not to disobey for the sake of disobedience but to grasp at divine prerogative without the divine authorization to use it. This frames the Fall as a usurpation rather than a moral failing in the simple sense.

⌘ Hebrew. Chavvah (חַוָּה) — ‘Eve.’ The name resembles the Hebrew word for ‘living’ (chai), and the text explicitly links them: she is ‘the mother of all living.’ Some scholars also note resonance with an Aramaic word for ‘serpent,’ suggesting a possible older mythological substrate, though this reading is contested.

⌘ Word. Baraʼ (בָּרָא) and the LXX choice. The Hebrew verb baraʼ in the Hebrew Bible takes only God as subject, never humans. The Septuagint renders it not with ktiżeīn (“to found / institute,” the verb used elsewhere for divine creation), but with epoīēsen (“made”, ἐποίησεν) — a more ordinary verb. The shift narrows the theological reach of the Hebrew. Genesis Rabbah 1:1 reads berešit as be-rešit, “by means of [wisdom]”, citing Proverbs 8:22; Philo similarly identifies the Logos as the instrument of creation.

⌘ Word. Ruḥ ʼElohim / pneuma theou. Hebrew ruḥ means breath, wind, or spirit; the LXX uses pneuma theou (πνεῦμα θεοῦ). The verb raḥef (hover) appears elsewhere only in Deut 32:11 of an eagle over its nest — a maternal image. Targum Onqelos translates “a wind from before the LORD,” preserving ambiguity; rabbinic readings (b. Ḥagigah 12a) imagine the Spirit as a brooding bird. Christian patristic tradition (Basil, Hexaemeron) reads this as the third person of the Trinity — a reading anachronistic for the original.

⌘ Word. Tanninim (תַּנִינִים) — sea-dragons. The Hebrew word renders elsewhere as “dragon” (Isa 27:1, Ezek 32:2), and is the same word used of Leviathan and Rahab. The LXX has kētē (κήτη), “great sea-monsters,” the same word used in the Greek Jonah for the swallowing fish. The Genesis 1 verse subtly demythologizes the chaos-monster tradition: in Mesopotamian myth Marduk slays Tiamat and her brood; here, God simply creates them and pronounces them good.

⌘ Word. Neshamah (נְשָׁמָה) vs. ruach. Two distinct “breath/spirit” words appear in Genesis 2: neshamat ḥayyim here (“breath of life,” LXX pnoēn zōēs), and ruḥ elsewhere. Rabbinic tradition (Genesis Rabbah 14:9) lists five distinct soul-words and reads neshamah as the highest. Note that the same breath enters all living creatures in 7:22 — it is not unique to humans, though humans alone receive it from God’s mouth directly.

⌘ Word. Gan / paradēisos (παράδεισος). The LXX renders gan (“garden”) with the Persian loan-word paradēisos, an enclosed royal park. From the Greek Bible the term entered Latin (paradisus) and became, in early Jewish and Christian usage, a name for the heavenly garden of the righteous (cf. Luke 23:43, 2 Cor 12:4, Rev 2:7). The Hebrew ʻeden may relate to Akkadian edinu (“plain”) or the Hebrew root for “delight”; the second etymology drives much rabbinic wordplay.

⌘ Word. Kerubim (כְּרוּבִים) and the Akkadian karibu. The cherubim are not the chubby winged children of Renaissance art; they are composite throne-attendants — lion-bodied, eagle-winged, human-faced (Ezek 1, 10). The Akkadian karibu denotes a guardian intercessor of the gods. The LXX preserves the Hebrew name as a loan-word: cheroubim. Their function as garden-guards parallels the colossal lamassu figures flanking Mesopotamian temple gates.

⌘ Hebrew. B’reshit (בְּרֵאשִׁית). The opening word names the entire book in Hebrew tradition. Grammatically a construct ("in the beginning of…") rather than absolute, leading some translators (Rashi, NRSV margin) to render "When God began to create" — turning v.1 into a temporal clause introducing v.2, with v.3 as the main action. The translation choice has theological consequences for the doctrine of creatio ex nihilo.

⌘ Hebrew. Ruaḥ Elohim m’raḥefet (רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים מְרַחֶפֶת). The verb raḥaf appears only one other place in the Pentateuch: Deut 32:11, where the eagle hovers over its young. The maternal-protective imagery is striking. Ruaḥ means simultaneously "breath," "wind," "spirit" — the same word that returns at Pentecost (Acts 2) when the Spirit "as of a rushing mighty wind" arrives.

◇ Note. The plural of self-deliberation. "Let us make" — the cohortative plural here has been read as: (1) a plural of majesty (royal we); (2) the divine council, God addressing the angelic host (1 Kings 22:19, Job 1–2, Ps 82); (3) Trinitarian foreshadowing (Christian tradition); (4) plural of self-address. The Targums avoid the difficulty by paraphrasing. The contrast with v.27 ("God created man in his own image" — singular) is itself part of the puzzle.

⌘ Hebrew. ‘Ezer k’negdo (עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ). Often translated "helper" with a connotation of subordination, but ‘ezer in the Hebrew Bible most commonly describes God’s help to Israel (Pss 33:20, 70:5, 121:1–2). The word carries no hierarchical weight. K’negdo means "corresponding to him" or "opposite him" — implying complementarity, equality, even confrontation. Closer to "counterpart" than "assistant."

◇ Note. The protoevangelium. Christian tradition since Irenaeus has read this verse as the first promise of redemption — the "first gospel," foreshadowing Christ’s victory over the serpent. The LXX renders the pronoun masculine (autos, "he"), strengthening the messianic reading; the Vulgate famously has ipsa ("she"), prompting the Marian reading still seen on icons. The Hebrew is gender-neutral — "the seed of the woman."

◇ Note. Day-pattern theology. Each creation day ends with this formula, marking the Hebrew pattern of evening-then-morning — the Jewish day begins at sunset (Lev 23:32). The contrast with most ancient Near Eastern creation accounts is deliberate: those typically privilege daybreak as cosmic origin. The sevenfold structure, with day-pairs (1/4 light, 2/5 sea-and-air, 3/6 land-and-animals, 7 rest), is a literary architecture identified by medieval Jewish commentators and given systematic modern treatment in Cassuto’s From Adam to Noah. The pattern frames each act of creation as bounded and complete rather than open-ended demiurgic activity.

◇ Note. Lights as time-markers. The luminaries are introduced functionally — not as objects of worship (as in Mesopotamian and Egyptian cult) but as temporal markers for the religious calendar. The Hebrew moʿadim (“appointed times”) designates the sacred festivals (Lev 23), and the same word organizes the temple service in Numbers 28–29. The theological move is polemical: Mesopotamian peoples worshiped Shamash (sun) and Sin (moon); Genesis demotes them to instruments of Israel’s calendar. Maimonides cites this passage in his polemic against astrolatry (Mishneh Torah, Avodah Zarah 1).

◇ Note. Polemic by omission. Strikingly, the text refuses to name sun and moon, using only “greater light” and “lesser light.” Most scholars read this as deliberate anti-pagan polemic: the Hebrew shemesh (sun) and yareach (moon) are also names of Mesopotamian and Canaanite deities. By withholding the names and assigning them subordinate functional roles (the verb mashal, “to govern,” is the language of any administrative authority), the author strips the heavenly bodies of divine status. The same demotion underlies Deuteronomy 4:19’s prohibition against worshiping the host of heaven.

◇ Note. First divine blessing. The first explicit blessing in Scripture (Hebrew peru u-revu, פְּרוּ וּרְבוּ) is given to sea creatures and birds, then repeated to humanity in 1:28. Rabbinic tradition derives the obligation of procreation from this verse (b. Yevamot 65b), debating whether it applies to women as well as men. The blessing’s grammatical form is intensive — both verbs in the imperative — suggesting not merely permission but mandate. The motif structures the patriarchal narratives, where procreation against barrenness becomes the locus of divine action (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah).

◇ Note Devotional application. The image-of-God claim places every human — across age, ability, ethnicity, status — within the same dignity. Practically: how we treat the person in front of us is a theological act, not merely a moral one. James 3:9 makes this explicit ("we curse men, who are made in God's image").

◇ Note Modern application: dominion and environment. The verb radah ("rule") has been read both as license for unrestrained exploitation and as a mandate for stewardship. Contemporary readings — across Jewish, Catholic (cf. Laudato Si', 2015), and Evangelical traditions — increasingly emphasize the latter, situating dominion within Gen 2:15's "till and keep" responsibility and the sabbath rhythm of Lev 25.

◇ Note. The culminating verdict. The phrase tov meʾod (“very good”) closes the creation week with a verdict stronger than the prior six “good”s. Most commentators, both Jewish (Rashi) and Christian (Aquinas, ST Ia.65.2), read this as an ontological affirmation of created reality — neither evil nor neutral but actively good. The phrase becomes foundational for theological positions resisting Manichaean and Gnostic dualism, which would identify matter or the material world with evil. The sevenfold tov structure is itself a literary achievement — six “good”s plus one “very good.”

◇ Note. Sabbath as cosmic structure. The seventh-day rest (Hebrew shavat) is not yet commanded as Israel’s Sabbath — that comes at Exodus 16 and 20 — but established as a pattern woven into creation itself. The conception of divine rest after labor parallels ancient Near Eastern creation accounts (Atrahasis, Enuma Elish), but with critical differences: the gods rest in those texts because humans were made to do their work; Genesis’s God rests in completion, not exhaustion. Heschel’s The Sabbath (1951) develops this as a “palace in time” — sanctity attached to the temporal rather than the spatial.

◇ Note. The first toledot. This is the first of ten toledot (תּוֹלְדוֹת) formulae that structure Genesis (2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10; 11:27; 25:12; 25:19; 36:1; 37:2). Each introduces a new genealogical or narrative section. That the first toledot belongs to “the heavens and the earth” — not to a person — establishes the cosmological frame: creation itself has a history. The literary structure has been central to documentary-source criticism (the formulae often signal source seams) and to canonical-narrative readings.

◇ Note. Sacred geography of the four rivers. Two of the four — the Hiddekel (Tigris) and Euphrates — are real and identifiable. The other two — Pishon flowing through Havilah, Gihon through Cush — are not certainly identifiable; proposals range from the Nile (Cush = Egypt/Ethiopia) to a Red Sea-area river system. The four-river symbolism evokes the cardinal directions and a primordial garden as the world’s source. Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22:1–2 invert the image: a single river flows out from the eschatological temple, restoring Eden’s centrality and reversing the multiplicity of dispersion.

◇ Note. One flesh. The phrase basar echad (בָּשָׂר אֶחָד) becomes foundational for Jewish and Christian marriage theology. Jesus cites this verse directly in Matthew 19:4–6 / Mark 10:6–9 against Mosaic permission for divorce, treating Genesis 2:24 as the original divine intent. Paul develops the motif in 1 Corinthians 6:16 (sexual union creates one-flesh status, even with a prostitute) and Ephesians 5:31 (marriage as type of Christ-Church union). The verse’s leaving-cleaving-becoming triad has been read in the rabbinic tradition as the three-fold structure of marriage itself (b. Kiddushin 41a).

◇ Note. Self-covering and shame. The first human action after disobedience is the manufacture of clothing — humans become the first textile-makers. The shift from “naked and not ashamed” (2:25) to “they knew that they were naked” introduces shame as a primary post-fall affect. The fig leaves’ inadequacy is suggested by God’s later substitution with garments of skin (3:21). Augustine’s reading (City of God XIV.17) develops this into a theology of post-fallen sexuality: shame indicates a loss of inner harmony between will and impulse, the body no longer obeying the rational soul as the soul no longer obeys God.

◇ Note. God walking. The image of God “walking” (Hebrew mithallek, מִתְהַלֵּךְ) in the garden suggests a form of divine immanence not preserved in later strata of biblical theology. The same verb describes God’s presence in the wilderness camp (Lev 26:12; Deut 23:14) and the “walking” of Enoch and Noah (Gen 5:22, 6:9 — both “walked with God”). Ancient interpreters were uncomfortable with the anthropomorphism; Targum Onkelos paraphrases as “the voice of the Word of the Lord” walking, removing the embodied imagery and substituting a hypostatized divine speech.

◇ Note. Divine interrogation. Three questions structure God’s interrogation of the fallen pair: “Where are you?” (3:9), “Who told you that you were naked?” (3:11), “What have you done?” (3:13). Most commentators (Brueggemann, Westermann) read these as pedagogical rather than informational — God knows where Adam is. The pattern of divine questioning persists into Cain (4:9 “Where is your brother?”) and Job 38–41 (the storm questions). Each reveals the questioner’s accountability rather than the questioner’s ignorance — interrogation as a mode of disclosure.

◇ Note. Curse on the serpent. The serpent receives the only direct curse in the Eden narrative — neither the woman nor Adam is cursed; only the ground and the serpent. The serpent’s “going on its belly” and “eating dust” are read by some commentators as etiological explanation of snake locomotion; by others as theological reduction — the serpent is demoted from upright/standing creature to permanent humiliation. Isaiah 65:25 picks up the imagery in its eschatological vision (“dust shall be the serpent’s food”), suggesting the curse persists into the new creation rather than being reversed.

◇ Note. First sacrifice / first provision. God’s clothing of Adam and Eve in animal skins is read in two major traditions. (1) As the first sacrifice — an animal had to die to clothe them, foreshadowing the substitutionary death of Levitical sacrifice and ultimately of Christ; this is a strong patristic reading (Justin, Athanasius). (2) As divine pastoral provision — God acts to ameliorate the consequences of sin even while imposing them, a model of mercy embedded within judgment; this is a stronger Jewish reading (Bereshit Rabbah 20:12). The two readings are not mutually exclusive.

‖ Parallel: Deut 32:11 / Matt 3:16. The hovering Spirit across the canon. The verb raḥaph (to hover, to brood) recurs in Deuteronomy 32:11, where the eagle hovers over its young — protective, generative motion. At Jesus' baptism in Matthew 3:16, the Spirit descends "like a dove," echoing this same brooding presence over the waters of a new creation. Early Christian readers (Tertullian, De Baptismo; Basil, Hexaemeron) saw baptism as a re-creation precisely on this typology.

‖ Parallel: John 1:1–3 / Heb 11:3 / Col 1:16–17. NT echoes of Genesis 1:1. Three foundational New Testament passages consciously rewrite this opening: John 1:1–3 ("In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God… all things were made through him") identifies the Logos as the divine agent of creation; Colossians 1:16–17 identifies that Logos as Christ ("by him all things were created… in him all things hold together"); and Hebrews 11:3 reads Genesis 1 as foundational for faith ("by faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God"). The repeated "and God said" of Genesis 1 is theologically reinterpreted as speech-acts of the pre-incarnate Word.

◇ Polemic against pre-existent matter. Genesis 1 against Mesopotamian cosmogony. Where the Babylonian Enuma Elish opens with primordial waters (Apsu and Tiamat) co-eternal with the gods and depicts creation as the violent dismemberment of Tiamat by Marduk, Genesis 1 begins with God already there and matter derivative. The Hebrew verb bara (always with God as subject) and the orderly speech-acts reframe creation as sovereign authorship rather than cosmic conflict. This is theological polemic, not borrowed mythology — Israel knew the surrounding cosmogonies and rejected their ontology of divine struggle.

‖ Parallel: 2 Cor 4:6 / John 1:5. The new-creation light. Paul reads Genesis 1:3 christologically in 2 Corinthians 4:6: "For God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." The first creative word becomes the template for the new creation in conversion. John 1:5 ("the light shines in the darkness") completes the pattern, with the incarnate Word now embodying the primal light.

◇ “After their kind” — categorical thinking. The phrase “after their kind” (Hebrew l'mino) recurs ten times in Genesis 1. It establishes the category of taxonomic stability — each life form reproduces within its own order. Jewish and Christian commentators across millennia (Philo, De Opificio; Aquinas, ST I.72) read this as the foundation for the doctrine of natural kinds: creation is not a chaos of undifferentiated potentiality but a structured cosmos of distinct natures. The repetition is liturgical, not redundant — it presses home the orderliness that Genesis 1 is asserting against mythological flux.

◇ Sun and moon demoted. Polemic by demotion. Genesis 1 conspicuously refuses to name the sun and moon — calling them merely "the greater light" and "the lesser light." In every surrounding culture (Egyptian Ra, Babylonian Shamash and Sin) the sun and moon were chief deities; here they are reduced to functional lamps, made on day four, subordinate to Israel's God. They "rule" only as appointed timekeepers. The same demotion underlies Deuteronomy 4:19's prohibition of astral worship.

⌘ Tselem and demut. Image (tselem) and likeness (demut). Two Hebrew terms covering overlapping ground. Tselem elsewhere denotes a physical statue or representation (used of idols in Numbers 33:52); demut denotes resemblance, pattern, or model. Most ANE kings claimed to be the "image" of their patron god — here Genesis democratizes royal language: every human bears divine representation. The Septuagint renders tselem as eikōn (icon), the same word Paul uses in Colossians 1:15 of Christ ("the image of the invisible God") and 2 Corinthians 4:4.

◇ The plural “let us”. The cohortative plural. "Let us make" (Hebrew na'aseh) is one of the great interpretive cruxes of Genesis. Patristic writers (Justin, Dialogue 62; Augustine, De Trinitate) read it as Trinitarian self-counsel; rabbinic readings (Genesis Rabbah 8:8) explored it as God consulting the angelic court (compare 1 Kings 22:19, Job 1:6); some moderns read it as a plural of majesty or self-deliberation. The Ethiopian Tewahedo tradition has historically read it Trinitarianly, consistent with its strong Christological reading of the Old Testament.

⌘ Yahweh Elohim — the compound name. Yahweh Elohim. Genesis 2:4b is where the personal divine name YHWH first appears in the Bible, and it appears in compound with Elohim — the more abstract or generic word for God used throughout Gen 1. The compound (occurring 20 times in Gen 2–3, then almost never again in the Pentateuch as a unit) signals theological identity: the cosmic Creator of chapter 1 (Elohim) is the same as the covenant God who breathes life into Adam (YHWH). The Targums and rabbinic tradition heard in this compound an early theological assertion of unity between the universal Creator and the particular God of Israel.

⌘ Yatsar — the potter shapes. The verb yatsar (to form, shape). Where Gen 1 used bara (sovereign creating) and asah (making), Gen 2:7 uses yatsar — the verb of a potter shaping clay, used elsewhere in Isaiah 29:16, 45:9, and Jeremiah 18:2–6 for God's plastic relation to humanity. The name Adam (adam) is wordplay on adamah (ground, soil) — the human is "earth-creature." Paul echoes this in Romans 9:21: God as potter and humanity as clay.

‖ Parallel: Rev 2:7 / Rev 22:2,14. The tree of life across the canon. The tree of life appears three times in Genesis (here, 3:22, 3:24) and then disappears from biblical narrative — until Revelation, where it reappears framing the entire eschatological vision: Revelation 2:7 ("to the one who conquers I will grant to eat of the tree of life"); 22:2 ("on either side of the river, the tree of life with its twelve kinds of fruit"); 22:14. Genesis 2 ends with humanity barred from the tree; Revelation 22 ends with humanity restored to it. The whole canon is bracketed by this single tree.

‖ Parallel: Ezek 28:13–14 / 31:8–9. Eden in Ezekiel. Ezekiel 28:13–14 recasts Eden as a jeweled mountain garden where a "guardian cherub" walks among "stones of fire" — the prophetic re-imagining of Eden, applied as oracle against the king of Tyre. Ezekiel 31:8–9 describes the trees of Eden envying Assyria's greatness. These passages preserve a different stream of Eden tradition — same garden, intensified imagery — and inform the apocalyptic imagery of Revelation 21–22.

‖ Parallel: Ezek 47:1–12 / Ps 46:4 / Rev 22:1. The river of Eden across the canon. The river that "goes out of Eden to water the garden" reappears in three crucial later texts: Ezekiel 47:1–12 sees a river flowing from the temple, making the sea fresh; Psalm 46:4 celebrates "a river whose streams make glad the city of God"; and Revelation 22:1 completes the pattern with "the river of the water of life… flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb." Eden's river becomes temple-river becomes new-creation-river.

◇ Death promised in the day of eating. "In the day… you will surely die." The Hebrew construction is the absolute infinitive followed by the finite verb — mot tamut — emphatic, sometimes rendered "dying you shall die." Adam and Eve do not physically die that day, prompting interpreters to read this as immediate spiritual death (separation from God), as inaugurating mortality, or as legal sentence (deserving death). Paul develops the legal-sentence reading in Romans 5:12 and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22: "in Adam all die." The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan interpolates that Adam's loss of divine glory was the immediate death; physical death was a delayed consequence.

◇ The first “not good” in creation. The first negative judgment. Until this verse, every assessment in the creation account has been "good" or "very good." Genesis 2:18 is the first "not good" — and it concerns aloneness. The narrative-theological force is that human isolation is itself a structural defect in creation, requiring the further work of Gen 2:21–24 to remedy. The early Greek Fathers (Chrysostom, Homilies on Genesis 15) read this as foundational for Christian anthropology: humans are made for communion, not solitude.

◇ Ezer kenegdo — strength corresponding. The phrase ezer kenegdo. Ezer is "help, ally, one who saves" — used 21 times in the Old Testament, 16 of them for God himself as helper of Israel (e.g., Deuteronomy 33:7; Psalm 33:20). Kenegdo means "corresponding to, opposite to, on a par with" — not subordinate but matched. The compound denies hierarchy in original creation: not "subordinate helper" but "strong ally facing him as equal." The Septuagint renders it boēthos (helper, deliverer), the same word the New Testament uses of God in Hebrews 13:6.

⌘ Tsela — rib or side. The word tsela. Translated "rib" but the Hebrew normally means "side" — used elsewhere of the side of the tabernacle (Exodus 25:12), the side of the temple (1 Kings 6:5), the side of a hill (2 Samuel 16:13). The architectural connotation matters: woman is taken not from man's foot or head but from his side — patristic and rabbinic readers (Augustine, City of God 22.17; Genesis Rabbah 18:2) press this image against both subordinationist and matriarchal readings.

‖ Parallel: Matt 19:5 / Mark 10:7–8 / Eph 5:31 / 1 Cor 6:16. Genesis 2:24 cited four times in the New Testament. Matthew 19:5 and Mark 10:7–8 — Jesus quotes this verse to ground marriage permanence ("what God has joined together, let no one separate"); Ephesians 5:31 — Paul cites it to ground the Christ-Church mystery; 1 Corinthians 6:16 — Paul cites it to argue against sexual immorality. No Old Testament verse is quoted by the New Testament more frequently in connection with marriage. The Ethiopian Tewahedo tradition has historically grounded its theology of marriage in this fourfold citation.

⌘ Arum / arumim — wordplay across chapters. The wordplay arum / arumim. Genesis 3:1 calls the serpent arum (subtle, crafty, cunning) — and Genesis 2:25 just before said the man and woman were arumim (naked). Identical Hebrew root, opposite valences. The reader hears: nakedness next to craftiness; vulnerability next to sly intelligence. After the fall (3:7) they realize they are erom (a different but assonant form for naked) and cover themselves. The wordplay frames the entire chapter as the loss of innocent transparency to the predation of cunning.

‖ Parallel: Rev 12:9 / Rev 20:2 / John 8:44. The serpent identified. Genesis 3 never names the serpent as anything beyond a created beast. The identification with Satan is later canonical synthesis: Revelation 12:9 ("the great dragon… the ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan"); Revelation 20:2 repeats this identification; John 8:44 calls the devil "a murderer from the beginning… the father of lies." The Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 ("through the devil's envy death entered the world") is among the earliest texts making the connection explicit.

‖ Parallel: Rom 16:20 / Heb 2:14 / Rev 12 — the protoevangelium. The "first gospel" (protevangelium). Patristic and Ethiopian Tewahedo tradition has long read Genesis 3:15 as the first messianic promise: the seed of the woman crushing the serpent's head prefigures Christ defeating Satan. New Testament echoes: Romans 16:20 ("the God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet"); Hebrews 2:14 ("through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil"); the entire war of Revelation 12, where the dragon pursues the woman and her seed. The Septuagint's use of masculine pronoun (autos "he") rather than expected neuter shaped Christological reading.

◇ The triple judgment of Eve, Adam, the serpent. The structure of the curses. Genesis 3:14–19 contains three judgments in chiastic order: serpent (3:14–15), woman (3:16), man (3:17–19). Each curse strikes at the central calling: the serpent at its means of motion, the woman at her uniquely reproductive role, the man at his work with the ground. Each also contains a glimmer of continued blessing: the woman will still bear children; the man will still eat bread. The fall does not erase the original blessing of Genesis 1:28 — it complicates it.

‖ Parallel: Rom 8:20–22 — creation in bondage. Creation's subjection to futility. Paul reads this curse as the foundation of his cosmic theology: Romans 8:20–22 — "the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it… the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now." The curse on the ground in Genesis 3 is, for Paul, not local but cosmic — and the redemption in view is not just human but creational. This becomes foundational for the new-creation theme of the New Testament.

◇ Knowing good and evil — what was lost?. What kind of knowledge? The phrase yada' tov va-ra (knowing good and evil) appears here, in 2:9, 2:17, and recurs in Deuteronomy 1:39 (children "who do not yet know good or evil"), 2 Samuel 19:35 (Barzillai "too old to discern"), and Isaiah 7:15. The expression denotes not moral knowledge in the abstract but adult moral discernment — the capacity to choose among options. The fall is therefore not the gaining of moral knowledge per se but the seizing of moral autonomy outside the structure of trust.

⌘ Cherubim — guardian beings. The cherubim. The plural kerubim appears 91 times in the Hebrew Bible. In Genesis 3 they guard the way to the tree of life. In Exodus 25:18–22 they are sculpted on the ark; in Ezekiel 1 and 10 they form God's mobile throne. ANE iconography (the winged sphinxes flanking Phoenician thrones, Assyrian karibu) suggests they are composite guardian beings, not the cherub-as-cute-baby of Renaissance art. Their function is consistent: bar unauthorized access to sacred space.
