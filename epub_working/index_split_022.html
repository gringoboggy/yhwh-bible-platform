Notes

◇ Note. The end of the Astronomical Book. 1 Enoch 72–82 (the so-called Book of the Heavenly Luminaries) preserves the 364-day solar calendar that anchors the Qumran community’s liturgical year. The Aramaic Astronomical Book (4QEnastrᵃ⁻ᵈ) is among the earliest layers of the Enoch tradition, possibly third century BCE. The same calendar is presupposed in Jubilees 6 and forms the framework of the Ethiopian liturgical year as preserved by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. The 364-day system is structurally divisible into four 91-day quarters and twelve 30-day months plus four intercalary days — a system designed to make every date fall on the same weekday each year.

◇ 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) (-150). 1 Enoch (Ethiopian tradition) Animal Apocalypse (1 Enoch 85-90) (c. 150 BC)

The Animal Apocalypse opens with Enoch's vision of human history under the symbolism of animals: 'I saw in my vision how a bull came forth from the earth, and that bull was white' — Adam as the white bull. The entire patriarchal-Mosaic-monarchic-exilic-Hasmonean history is then narrated in animal allegory (humans as animals, angels as men, the elect as sheep, the wicked as wolves and other predators). This is the most extensive surviving pre-Christian Jewish historical-prophetic allegory; its inclusion in the Tewahedo canon gives the Ethiopian Christian historical imagination a unique typological grammar that Western Christianity (lacking the text) cannot access.

Tewahedo homilies on Cain/Abel preserve this colour-coded allegorical structure.

◇ Note. The Animal Apocalypse begins. 1 Enoch 85–90 retells history with each people as an animal: Adam and Seth are white bulls; Cain a black bull; Israel’s patriarchs cattle and sheep; the Gentile nations beasts of prey. The whiteness of the first bulls signals primordial purity, lost as the genealogy darkens. The composition almost certainly belongs to the Maccabean crisis (mid-second century BCE), with the “white bull” at the climax (90:37) widely read as the messianic figure. It is one of the earliest sustained allegorical histories in any tradition.

◇ Note. The Apocalypse of Weeks. 1 Enoch 93:1–10 + 91:11–17 originally formed a single composition that divides world history into ten weeks, with the seventh marking the apostasy of the present age and the tenth ending in eschatological judgment. The Aramaic fragments (4QEnᵍ 1 iv) preserve this structure against the slightly disordered Geʻez transmission. The ten-week scheme parallels the seventy-week scheme of Daniel 9:24–27 — both are early experiments in periodizing redemptive history. It is a precursor to later Christian millenarian schemes.

◇ Note. The Woes (1 Enoch 94–105). The Epistle of Enoch contains a sustained set of “woe” oracles directed at the wealthy, the violent, and those who oppress the righteous — the most concentrated social-justice polemic in any Second Temple Jewish text. The vocabulary of plousios (rich) and pénes (poor) in the Greek closely anticipates the New Testament epistle of James (James 5:1–6), which appears to draw directly on this Enochic tradition. The Aramaic fragments (4QEnᵍ 1 ii) confirm the pre-Christian dating of the woes.

◇ Note. The birth of Noah — 1 Enoch 106–107. Lamech’s strange newborn son frightens him: the child has eyes that shine, hair white as snow, and the appearance of an angel rather than a human. Lamech suspects his wife of conceiving by a Watcher. The same scene appears in the Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen 2–5), where Lamech’s wife Bitenosh swears the child is his by tearful oath. The Aramaic of 4QEnᵉ 5 confirms the antiquity of this tradition. It is the bridge that knits the Enochic Watchers narrative back into the Genesis flood story — Noah is born into a world still partially controlled by the giants’ descendants, and his birth is itself contested.

◇ Note. The frame: Enoch instructing Methuselah. Standard frame device of the Dream Visions and the Epistle that follows. Enoch as antediluvian sage transmits esoteric knowledge to his descendants — an authority claim grounding the appeal of the entire Enochic tradition. Compare Jubilees 4:21–25, where Methuselah receives Enoch’s astronomical writings. The convention extends across Second Temple literature: dying-patriarch farewells coupled with eschatological revelation appear in the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs and the Testament of Moses. Nickelsburg observes that the Dream Visions section opens precisely on this generic note — signalling the move into apocalyptic mode.

◇ Note. Cosmic-collapse imagery. The undoing of Genesis 1: hills sink, mountains shake, trees uprooted. Decreation is the standard apocalyptic shorthand for divine judgment, threading through Isaiah 24, Jeremiah 4:23–26 (where tohu va-vohu is re-imposed on the land), Joel 2:30–31, and the Synoptic Apocalypse (Mark 13:24–25). The terror prepares for chapter 84’s intercessory prayer — Enoch sees the flood coming and pleads for mercy on a righteous remnant.

◇ Note. Petition answered by the Animal Apocalypse. Enoch petitions for preservation of "the plant of righteousness." The prayer is structurally answered when chapter 85 opens with the white bull (Adam) and the apocalypse proceeds through Noah’s deliverance and the unbroken righteous line. The "plant" metaphor for the elect community recurs throughout the Epistle of Enoch (93:2, 5, 10) and at Qumran (1QS 8:5; 1QH 14:15) — a sectarian self-designation tying back to Isaiah 60:21 and 61:3.

◇ Note. The Animal Apocalypse begins. Chapters 85–90 present world history as zoological allegory. The white bull is Adam; the heifer that follows is Eve. Bovines for the antediluvian patriarchs marks them as clean, sacrificial-grade animals (cf. Leviticus 1, Numbers 19); the shift to sheep at Jacob (89:12) marks the election of Israel. Clean-versus-unclean animal symbolism encodes covenant status throughout. The decisive scholarly studies are Patrick Tiller, A Commentary on the Animal Apocalypse (1993), and Daniel Olson, A New Reading of the Animal Apocalypse (2013).

◇ Note. Cain (black) and Abel (red). Color-coding signals moral character: black for wickedness, red for blood and martyrdom. The conventions hold throughout the apocalypse — white for righteous, black for wicked, red for the slaughtered righteous. The black bull "gored" the red — the same Hebrew imagery (nagaḥ) used in Exodus 21:28 for fatal goring oxen. Nickelsburg notes the polemical edge: moral status is visible from the start, cutting against any reading of moral indifferentism in early human history.

◇ Note. The first Watcher’s fall — Asael / Azazel. Stars stand for angelic beings throughout the Enochic corpus (compare ch 18, where seven stars are bound for breaking their assigned courses). This first falling star is Asael, leader of the Watchers in the Book of Watchers (chs 6–11). The sequence of one-then-many falls from heaven (86:1, 86:3) recapitulates the Watchers narrative in animal form, showing the Animal Apocalypse’s continuity with the Enochic corpus’s opening section.

The reversal of Malachi 4:6 (“he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children, and the hearts of children to their fathers”) — apocalyptic crisis pulls families apart rather than reconciling them. The same motif appears in Micah 7:6 (“the son treats the father with contempt”) and Jesus’ citation of it in Matthew 10:35 / Luke 12:53. Family disintegration as eschatological sign is a recurrent apocalyptic trope, signaling the dissolution of the most basic social bonds before the day of judgment.

◇ Note. Wisdom-appeal to creation. The chapter shifts to a wisdom-meditation on the order of nature: heaven, sea, weather are observed, and the human is exhorted to draw moral conclusions. The form parallels Job 38–41 and Psalm 104. The conception that natural order grounds moral order persists into Romans 1:19–20 and is foundational for later natural-theology traditions. The Epistle here speaks not as apocalyptic seer but as wisdom-teacher.

◇ Note. Sailors’ fear. The image of sailors trembling before storm-tossed seas draws on Psalm 107:23–32 (“some went down to the sea in ships … they reeled and staggered like drunken men, and were at their wits’ end”). The Epistle uses the sailors’ instinctive religiosity as moral exempla: those whose livelihood depends on the sea fear God; how much more should sinners on dry land. The same rhetorical move appears in Jonah 1 (the pagan sailors’ fear shaming Jonah).

◇ Note. Fire of judgment. Eschatological fire as the medium of judgment is a Second Temple development of older imagery (Isa 66:15–16; Mal 4:1). The Epistle deploys the fire-motif at the climax of its woe-sequence. 2 Peter 3:7–12 (“the heavens and earth that now exist are stored up for fire, being kept until the day of judgment”) and 2 Thessalonians 1:7–8 inherit this register directly. The shift from “sword of judgment” (older prophetic) to “fire of judgment” (apocalyptic) is one of the markers of the Second Temple development.

◇ Note. Heavenly tablets revisited. The motif from 81:1 returns: Enoch’s authority rests on his having read the heavenly tablets, where the destinies of righteous and wicked are inscribed before time. The conception of pre-existing tablets containing all of history influences Jubilees (where the entire Pentateuch is read off heavenly tablets) and survives into rabbinic notions of the pre-existent Torah. The Pauline “mystery hidden for ages” (Rom 16:25; Col 1:26) operates in the same conceptual framework: hidden divine plans now revealed.

◇ Note. Book of remembrance. The motif of angelic memorial-keeping draws on Malachi 3:16 (“a book of remembrance was written before him of those who feared the Lord and esteemed his name”). The Epistle assures the righteous that despite earthly oppression, they are inscribed in heavenly records. Hebrews 12:23 (“the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven”) and Revelation 13:8 (“whose names have not been written before the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain”) develop the same complex of motifs.

◇ Note. Shining as the heavenly lights. Direct echo of Daniel 12:3. Matthew 13:43 deploys the same image with the same eschatological weight (“the righteous will shine like the sun”). The astronomical-luminary metaphor for resurrection-glory is one of the most consistent throughlines from Daniel through 1 Enoch into the New Testament — the righteous transformed not into a different kind of being but into a glorified embodiment whose paradigm is the celestial luminaries.

◇ Note. Testifying wisdom. The two-verse chapter functions as the formal close of the Epistle proper before the appended Noachic material (chs 106–107) and the second appendix (ch 108). Wisdom is not private but to be testified — the righteous are commissioned as guides for “the children of earth.” The same motif of received-wisdom-as-public-teaching structures Matthew 5:14 (“you are the light of the world”) and 1 Peter 2:9 (“that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness”).

◇ Note. “I and My son.” The verse is one of the most exegetically debated in 1 Enoch. Three readings: (1) “My son” = the Messiah (the Elect One / Son of Man of the Parables); (2) “My son” = Methuselah, addressed in the immediate narrative frame; (3) the verse is a Christian interpolation. The Christian-interpolation thesis (popular in the 19th century) is now generally rejected, since the Aramaic and Ethiopic textual evidence does not support a late insertion. The reading is probably messianic — but the Parables-Epistle continuity in messianic vocabulary makes the identification ambiguous in revealing ways: the messianic Son of Man is the divine Son, not a third figure.

◇ Note. Generation of righteousness. The eschatological hope: a future generation will appear that breaks the cycle of transgression, after which sin will pass from the earth. The schema parallels the “generation that shall not pass away” of Mark 13:30 — the eschatological generation that witnesses the consummation. The Qumran community read itself as standing on the threshold of this turning generation; early Christians read the church as constituted by it (Phil 2:15 “a crooked and twisted generation, among whom you shine as lights”).

◇ Note. Etymology of Noah. The name “Noah” (Nōaḥ) is etymologically connected with the Hebrew root nḥm (“to comfort”) — though the more direct etymological connection is to nwḥ (“to rest”). The interplay underlies Genesis 5:29 (“This one shall bring us relief from our work and from the painful toil of our hands”). The Epistle preserves the comfort-etymology. The naming-with-etymology pattern (the child’s name encoding his eschatological role) is generic to biblical birth-narratives (Isaac, Israel, Immanuel, Jesus) and supplies one of the templates for the Lukan and Matthean infancy narratives.

◇ Note. Appendix attribution. Ch 108 is the second appendix to the Enochic corpus (the first being chs 106–107, the Noachic birth narrative). The opening — “Another book” — explicitly marks ch 108 as a separate composition appended to the Epistle. The Aramaic Qumran fragments do not preserve ch 108, suggesting it may be a later Greek/Ethiopic addition. Charles classified it as a “summary book” gathering apocalyptic motifs into a closing meditation on judgment, the righteous, and the place of punishment.

◇ Note. Blotted out of the book of life. The motif draws on Psalm 69:28 (“let them be blotted out of the book of the living”) and Exodus 32:32–33. The conception inverts the “names written” motif of 104:1: where the righteous are inscribed for remembrance, the unrighteous are erased from the registers of the living. Revelation 3:5 explicitly engages this tradition: “the one who conquers … I will never blot his name out of the book of life” — the Christian text presupposes the Enochic vocabulary it inverts toward promise.

◇ Note. Luxury and gender inversion. The verse uses gender-coded clothing imagery to critique luxury: men wearing more adornment than women is read as a sign of moral disorder. The cultural assumption — that excess ornamentation is feminine and that men adopting it inverts the moral order — reflects ancient Mediterranean social codes more than any timeless ethic. Yet the underlying critique of conspicuous consumption transcends its cultural framing and parallels James 2:2–4 (the rich man with gold rings entering the assembly) and 1 Timothy 2:9 (modesty in adornment).

◇ Note. Creation as witness. The chapter closes with creation itself summoned as forensic witness against the wicked: cloud, mist, dew, rain testify because they have been “mindful of your sins.” The trope draws on Deuteronomy 30:19 (“I call heaven and earth to witness against you today”) and Isaiah 1:2 (“hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth”). The Epistle radicalizes the older covenant-witness motif: not only the sky and earth in general but each meteorological element individually testifies. The anthropomorphizing of creation as moral witness persists into Romans 8:19–22 (creation groaning) and Revelation 11:6 (the witnesses with power over weather).

◇ Note. Companions of sinners. The verse pronounces a striking forensic verdict: those who associate with sinners share their fate (“Ye have been companions of sinners”). The principle of guilt-by-association anticipates Paul’s “bad company ruins good morals” (1 Cor 15:33, citing Menander) and 1 Timothy 5:22’s warning not to “take part in the sins of others.” The Epistle of Enoch frames the ethic apocalyptically: companionship with the wicked is itself a forensic act with eschatological consequence.

◇ Note. Oath-formula intensification. The Epistle’s frequent use of swearing-language (also 98:4, 6; 99:6; 103:1; 104:1) gives the prophetic register a forensic-legal weight: the seer testifies under oath that the revelation is true. The structure resembles Hebrews 6:13–18, where God’s oath-bound promise grounds Christian assurance. Matthew 5:33–37’s Synoptic prohibition on oath-swearing is a counter-tradition: the Synoptic Jesus reverses the apocalyptic intensification of swearing.

◇ Note. Godlessness as foundational sin. The opening woe of ch 99 names “godlessness” (Greek likely asebeia) as the root category, paired with “glorying in lying.” Romans 1:18 uses the same vocabulary (asebeia kai adikia, “godlessness and unrighteousness”) to introduce its diagnosis of the human condition. The Epistle’s combination — godlessness paired with lying — anticipates the Johannine pairing in 1 John 2:4 (“whoever says ‘I know him’ but does not keep his commandments is a liar”). Falsehood and irreligion are linked at the root.

◇ Note. The denial of eudaimonia. The verse closes with the negation of any future flourishing for the wicked: “no happy life shall be yours.” The phrase echoes the wisdom-tradition refrain that the prosperity of the wicked is illusory (Ps 73; Job 21). In Hellenistic-Jewish vocabulary, “happy life” renders bios eudaimōn — the philosophical good life of Aristotelian-Stoic tradition. The Epistle thus turns Greek philosophical vocabulary against the wicked: the very condition the philosophers sought is denied to them by apocalyptic verdict.

◇ Note. Cosmic dismay. The eschatological vision pictures the heavenly luminaries themselves as frightened by divine judgment. The motif of cosmic mourning before the day of the Lord is biblical (Isa 13:10; Joel 2:10, 31; Amos 8:9) and culminates in Mark 13:24–25 (“the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will be falling from heaven”). The Epistle uses it to introduce the climactic promise of the righteous dead’s vindication.
