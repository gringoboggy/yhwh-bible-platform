◇ Note. Solomon’s apostasy. The Deuteronomistic verdict on Solomon: the king who built Yahweh’s house also built shrines to Chemosh, Molech, and Ashtoreth for his foreign wives. The political-economic foundations of the Solomonic empire (foreign alliances cemented by marriage) are exposed as theological fault lines. Every queen brought her gods.

◇ Note. The kingdom’s tearing. ◇ Note. Jezebel of Tyre. Daughter of Ethbaal (Ittobaal I, c. 887–856 BCE), king-priest of Sidon. Her name Izevel is the subject of probable scribal mockery — the original may have been theophoric (compare Phoenician I-Ba'al, "where is Baal") and was likely revocalized to suggest zevel ("dung") or ze'evel ("not exalted"). Her arc through 1 Kgs 16 – 2 Kgs 9 is the spine of the Elijah/Elisha narrative complex.

◇ Note. Rehoboam’s threat. "Scorpions" likely refers to a scourge with knotted barbs designed to tear flesh. Rehoboam’s reply to the northern tribes’ grievance over Solomonic forced labor is one of the great folly-set-pieces of scripture: he rejects the elder counsel, takes the young men’s advice, and triples down on the corvée. The kingdom splits the next verse. The narrative pattern — wise old advice rejected for young flattery — appears again in Greek tragedy and Shakespeare; the biblical version reads as a foundational political parable. The forced labor of the temple-building era was literally building toward this fracture.

◇ Note. The widow of Zarephath. Yahweh feeds the prophet not in Israelite territory but in Phoenician Sidon — Jezebel’s home country. The provision-from-nothing motif (handful of meal, little oil, neither runs out) parallels Elisha’s widow’s oil (2 Kgs 4:1–7) and is invoked by Jesus in Luke 4:25–26 to make a controversial point: the prophet was sent past the widows of Israel to a foreigner. ◇ Note. The kavod fills the temple. The Hebrew kavod ("glory," "weight") is the visible-perceivable presence of Yahweh, paralleled in Exod 40:34–35 (the tabernacle) and Ezek 10–11 (where the kavod departs from this same temple before its destruction). The cloud-filling scene establishes the temple as legitimate dwelling-place; Ezekiel’s departure scene establishes that legitimacy as forfeited. Both scenes use the same vocabulary deliberately. The Christian gospel of John intentionally echoes this idiom — "the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and we beheld his glory" (John 1:14) — claiming the same divine indwelling for Christ.
